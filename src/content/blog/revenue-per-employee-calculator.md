**Revenue per employee equals total revenue divided by average full time equivalents over the same period. It measures operating leverage, meaning how much output your current staffing structure supports, not individual productivity. Track it against your own prior periods with a frozen headcount definition before you compare it to any peer benchmark.**

## What revenue per employee actually measures

**Revenue per employee is a leverage ratio, not a performance score. It moves when revenue mix, pricing, outsourcing, or headcount classification change, and none of those are the same as people working harder. Read it as a question about structure: how much revenue does your current operating setup carry per full time seat?**

Three changes can lift the number without a single person becoming more effective:

- **A price increase.** Same work, same staff, higher revenue. The ratio rises entirely on the numerator.
- **Reclassifying labor.** Move three roles to a contractor agency and your FTE count drops while the work continues. Revenue per employee improves and total labor cost may not.
- **Dropping a low ticket service line.** Revenue falls a little, headcount falls more, the ratio goes up.

That last point is the reason to be careful with causation language. Revenue per employee tells you the shape of your cost structure. It does not tell you why the shape changed, and it never isolates one team's contribution. Pair it with a margin metric before you draw a conclusion, because outsourcing at a premium raises this ratio while shrinking profit.

## The formula and the FTE denominator

**Revenue per employee equals period revenue divided by average FTE for that exact period. The denominator does the damage: paid hours, not headcount, and one consistent rule for contractors, part timers, and owners who also work billable hours. Write the definition down before you calculate anything.**

```
Revenue per employee = Total revenue for the period / Average FTE for the same period

Average FTE = Total paid hours in the period / (Standard weekly hours x Weeks in period)
```

A 40 hour standard and a 52 week year gives 2,080 paid hours per FTE. Two people at 20 hours a week are one FTE, not two employees. If you count heads instead of hours, every part time hire makes your business look worse than it is.

| Denominator choice | What it includes | Use it when |
|---|---|---|
| Average FTE from paid hours | W2 staff, prorated by hours | Default. Most stable across seasons |
| Point in time headcount | Bodies on payroll at period end | Never, if you have any part time staff |
| FTE plus contractor FTE equivalent | Contractor hours divided by 2,080 | Delivery leans heavily on contractors |
| Revenue generating FTE only | Excludes admin and back office | You are measuring sales capacity, not the whole business |

Pick one and stay with it. Building the paid hours side properly is its own exercise, and the [FTE calculator: turn workload into real capacity](/blog/fte-calculator/) walks through converting scheduled workload into hours you can defend. ## A worked example with illustrative numbers

**Here is a two year read for an illustrative 18 person plumbing company. Revenue rose 15 percent while average FTE fell from 18.0 to 17.5, so revenue per employee climbed about 18 percent. Decomposing the change shows roughly a third of it came from a price increase, not from staffing leverage.**

All figures below are illustrative, built to show the arithmetic rather than to represent any real company.

| Line | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Total revenue | $2,400,000 | $2,760,000 |
| Average FTE | 18.0 | 17.5 |
| Revenue per employee | $133,333 | $157,714 |
| Change | | +18.3% |

Now strip out pricing. Year 2 included a 6 percent price increase. At Year 1 prices, revenue would have been about $2,603,774, giving revenue per employee of $148,787, a gain of 11.6 percent. So of the 18.3 point improvement, about 6.7 points came from pricing and about 11.6 points came from doing the same volume with half a seat fewer.

That decomposition is the whole point. Without it, an owner reports an 18 percent productivity jump. With it, the honest sentence is that structure improved by roughly 12 percent and pricing did the rest.

## Comparing pre and post, and comparing to peers

**A comparison is only worth reading when the definition on both sides is identical. Freeze the revenue basis, the FTE rule, and the period length, then compare yourself to your own prior year first. Peer benchmarks are useful only when the source publishes its denominator, which most do not.**

Run this checklist before you put two numbers side by side:

1. **Same revenue basis.** Recognized revenue both periods, or booked both periods. Never one of each.
2. **Same FTE rule.** If contractors were excluded last year, exclude them this year, even if the mix changed.
3. **Same period length.** Compare a full year to a full year. Quarterly reads swing on seasonality alone.
4. **Same entity scope.** An acquisition or a closed location breaks the series. Start a new one.
5. **Note structural events in the same row.** A layoff, a price change, a dropped service line, a new location.
6. **Check turnover before you celebrate.** A falling FTE count that comes from churn is not leverage.

Point six catches more people than the other five combined. Average FTE drops when roles go unfilled, revenue holds for a quarter on backlog, and the ratio looks great right before delivery slips. ## Connecting reclaimed capacity to the calculator

**Reclaimed hours change the denominator only if you actually convert them into fewer paid hours or into billable work. Convert hours to FTE first, then decide which of the two happens. Automation that frees 1,144 hours a year is 0.55 FTE at 2,080 paid hours, and nothing more until you act on it.**

The conversion is simple. If automating intake, reminders, and after hours call handling saves an illustrative 22 hours a week across the team, that is 1,144 hours a year, or 0.55 FTE. Applied to the Year 2 example above, moving from 17.5 to 16.95 average FTE would lift revenue per employee to about $162,832 at flat revenue.

That result depends on three conditions, and skipping any of them makes the projection fiction:

- Demand exists for the freed hours, or you genuinely reduce paid hours.
- The saved time is contiguous enough to be usable. Ninety seconds recovered forty times a day is not the same as half a day.
- Someone reassigns the capacity on purpose. Unassigned slack is absorbed, not banked.

## When revenue per employee is the wrong metric

**Skip this metric when headcount is small enough that one hire swings it double digits, when a large share of delivery runs through contractors, or when revenue is seasonal and your FTE count is not. In those cases gross profit per FTE answers the same question with less noise.**

At eight FTE, adding one person moves the ratio 11 percent on its own. The signal is buried in hiring timing. Pass through revenue creates the same problem: a business that resells hardware at thin margin will post a strong revenue per employee number and a weak business. Divide gross profit by average FTE instead, and the reseller distortion disappears.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is revenue per employee the same as employee productivity?

No. It is a structural ratio that changes with pricing, revenue mix, outsourcing, and how you classify labor. Two companies with identical staff effectiveness will post different numbers if one sells a higher ticket service. Use it to track your own operating leverage over time, not to grade people.

### Should contractors count in the FTE denominator?

Include them if they perform work that would otherwise sit on payroll, converting their hours to FTE equivalents by dividing by 2,080. Exclude them if they are genuinely project based. Either rule works. Switching rules between periods does not, because it manufactures a change that never happened.

### What is a good revenue per employee number?

There is no universal target, and published benchmarks are usually unusable because the source rarely states whether it used headcount or FTE, gross or net revenue. Your own trailing four quarters, calculated the same way each time, is a more reliable comparison than any external figure.

### How often should I recalculate it?

Annually for the headline number and quarterly as a trend line, using a rolling twelve month revenue figure to strip out seasonality. Monthly reads on a small team mostly measure payroll timing.

### Can automation raise revenue per employee?

It can, through either side of the ratio: fewer paid hours for the same revenue, or the same hours redirected to revenue generating work. It does neither automatically. The gain only appears when reclaimed capacity is deliberately reassigned or removed from the payroll hour count.
