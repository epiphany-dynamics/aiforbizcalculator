**Absenteeism rate is the share of scheduled work time lost to unplanned absence. Divide absent hours by total scheduled hours, multiply by 100. It measures lost scheduled time only, not turnover and not productivity. Tracking it monthly gives small teams an early warning signal before staffing and cost problems show up in payroll.**

## What the absenteeism rate formula actually measures

**The standard formula is absent hours divided by scheduled hours, times 100. Scheduled hours means every hour you planned a person to work, including hours they missed. The result is a percentage of planned capacity that never happened. It says nothing about why people left the company or how fast the present employees work.**

The formula in plain text:

Absenteeism rate = (Absent hours / Total scheduled hours) x 100

Three definitions keep this honest:

1. Absent hours: unplanned missed time, such as sick calls, no-shows, and late arrivals beyond a grace window you define.
2. Scheduled hours: every hour on the roster for the period, whether worked or not.
3. Period: pick one and stick to it. Monthly works best for teams under 50 people because quarterly smooths out spikes you actually want to see.

What stays out of the numerator matters as much as what goes in. Planned time off, approved vacation, holidays, and jury duty are scheduled-away hours, not absences. If you count approved PTO as absence, your rate inflates and you end up solving a problem that does not exist.

A clean alternative is counting in days instead of hours. Days absent divided by scheduled days works for teams on fixed full-time shifts. Hours are better when you run part-time staff or variable shifts, because a 4-hour absence in a 10-hour week is a different event than the same absence in a 40-hour week.

## Worked example: a 14-person team over one month

**Illustrative example: a 14-person service business schedules 2,240 hours in a month. Employees miss 78 hours unplanned. The absenteeism rate is 78 divided by 2,240, times 100, which is 3.5 percent. That is 78 hours of paid capacity that produced nothing, before anyone discusses why.**

Here is the full breakdown. All numbers below are illustrative, not benchmarks.

| Input | Value | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Headcount | 14 | Mix of full-time and part-time |
| Scheduled hours per person | 160 | Average across the month |
| Total scheduled hours | 2,240 | 14 x 160 |
| Unplanned absent hours | 78 | Sick calls, no-shows, early departures |
| Approved PTO hours | 120 | Excluded from absence |
| Absenteeism rate | 3.5% | 78 / 2,240 x 100 |

Notice what the 120 PTO hours do not do: they do not enter the absence numerator. If someone miscounted them as absence, the rate would jump to 8.8 percent and trigger a panic over a normal vacation month.

Now run the same team a second month where absent hours rise to 134. The rate moves to 6.0 percent. That single change, tracked month over month, is the actual product of this calculation. One month's number is a snapshot. Three months of numbers is a trend, and trends are what let you act before the problem compounds.

## Why absenteeism is not turnover and not productivity

**Absenteeism, turnover, and productivity measure three separate failures. Absenteeism is scheduled time that did not happen. Turnover is people leaving and being replaced. Productivity is output per hour actually worked. An employee can be absent rarely, work slowly, and still quit in June. Mixing the three hides which fix you need.**

Owners routinely collapse these into one bucket called "people problems" and then apply the wrong remedy. If absence is high but output per worked hour is fine, the fix is scheduling, coverage, or attendance policy, not training. If the rate is low but output lags, absence is not your issue at all.

The three metrics do interact. Chronic absence in one reliable employee often precedes resignation, and covering absent shifts burns out the people who show up, which later shows up as turnover. Track them separately, read them together.

## What to count and what to exclude

**Count unplanned absences in hours: sick calls, no-shows, unexcused late arrivals, and early departures past your grace window. Exclude approved vacation, holidays, bereavement policy days, jury duty, and planned medical leave. Write your inclusion rules down once, because inconsistent counting makes month-to-month comparisons worthless.**

Use this checklist before your first calculation:

- Define your grace window for lateness, such as 15 minutes, and apply it to everyone.
- Decide whether partial absences count in hours or round to half days.
- Exclude all pre-approved time off, no exceptions.
- Decide how to handle remote workers and apply the same rule.
- Pick a source of truth: time clock data beats manager memory.
- Set one calculation date per month so the period never shifts.

The most common counting error is mixing data sources. If January comes from the time clock and February comes from a supervisor's spreadsheet, the change in the rate may reflect the source change rather than any real change in attendance.

The second error is double-counting. If an employee calls in sick for a full shift, that is one absence event and 8 absent hours, not 8 absence events. Whether you track events or hours, pick one unit and stay in it.

## Turning the rate into a dollar figure

**Multiply absent hours by the fully loaded labor rate to get the direct cost of absence. That rate includes wages, payroll taxes, benefits, and employer contributions, not just the hourly wage. Then add coverage costs such as overtime paid to whoever filled the gap. For the illustrative example above, 78 hours at $31 loaded is about $2,418 in one month.**

The direct-cost formula:

Monthly absence cost = Absent hours x Fully loaded hourly rate

The fully loaded rate matters because the wage alone understates the loss by 20 to 40 percent for most employers. The [Fully loaded labor rate: how to calculate yours](/blog/fully-loaded-labor-rate/) shows how to build that number from your own payroll data instead of guessing a multiplier.

On top of the direct cost, coverage adds a second line. If filling those 78 hours required 60 hours of overtime at 1.5 times a $25 wage, that is another $2,250, and the absent employees may have been paid sick time on top of it. Suddenly a 3.5 percent absence rate costs more than $5,000 a month for a 14-person team, all illustrative but all arithmetic you can rerun with your own numbers.

If absence-driven overtime is a recurring cost, you can model whether better scheduling automation or reminder workflows pay back against it, using the same hours-times-rate logic you just ran.

## One limitation: small numbers lie easily

**On very small teams, one employee's bad month swings the rate dramatically and the metric overreacts. With 6 employees, a single 40-hour absence in a month moves the rate roughly 4 points by itself. For teams under 10, track absent hours alongside the rate and read rolling three-month averages instead of single months.**

This is the honest caveat: the absenteeism rate is a ratio, and ratios with small denominators are noisy. A 5-person shop where one person has surgery is not experiencing an attendance crisis, but the monthly rate will scream otherwise.

Two safeguards help. First, report absent hours next to the percentage so scale stays visible. Second, use a rolling three-month window for any decision, and keep the single-month figure only as an alert. If your team is under 5 people, the formula still works but a simple list of absence events per person may tell you more than the percentage does.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a normal absenteeism rate for a small business?

There is no single durable number that fits every industry, shift pattern, and PTO policy, and any source quoting one is usually describing a specific sector. The more reliable approach is to establish your own baseline over three to six months, then watch for sustained movement above it. A rate that doubles from your own baseline is a signal regardless of what any outside average says.

### Should I count sick days as absenteeism?

Unplanned sick calls, yes, because they are scheduled time lost without warning. Pre-scheduled medical leave or approved extended sick leave, no, because you could plan coverage around it. The distinction that matters is planned versus unplanned, not the reason for the absence. Write the rule down and apply it identically every month.

### Is absenteeism rate the same as turnover rate?

No. Absenteeism measures scheduled hours lost while people are still employed. Turnover measures people leaving the company entirely, calculated as separations divided by average headcount. An employee who quits stops contributing to absenteeism and starts contributing to turnover. Track both, because high absence in one person often precedes a resignation.

### How often should I calculate the absenteeism rate?

Monthly is the practical cadence for most small businesses. Weekly creates too much noise on small teams, and quarterly smooths away spikes you want to catch, such as a pattern of Monday absences in one department. Calculate monthly, review a rolling three-month average for decisions, and investigate any month that lands well above your baseline.

### Can I calculate absenteeism rate without time clock software?

Yes, as long as you have schedules and a consistent record of who missed what. A shared spreadsheet logging each unplanned absence in hours, updated the day it happens, is enough. The risk is recall bias if managers fill it in at month end. Same-day logging matters more than the tool you log it in.

### What do I do if my absenteeism rate is climbing?

First, split the data by person, shift, and day of week to find where the hours concentrate. Concentrated absence points to a specific situation; spread-out absence points to a policy or morale issue. Then cost it with your loaded labor rate so you know what the trend costs per month, and use the [AI automation ROI calculator](/calculator/) to test whether scheduling, reminder, or coverage automation pays back against that figure.

The next step: pull last month's schedules and absence records, run the formula, and put the rate and the dollar cost on one line of your monthly review. Once absence is a number with a price tag, it stops being a vague frustration and becomes a decision you can actually make.
