**After-hours coverage cost equals your total spend on nights and weekends divided by the hours covered, and most small businesses can choose between three models: on-call pay for staff, a live answering service, or automation. This guide gives you the formula, illustrative numbers for each model, and a decision framework for picking the cheapest option that still captures revenue.**

## What counts as after-hours coverage cost

**After-hours coverage cost includes every dollar you spend to respond to customers outside your normal schedule: on-call stipends, overtime premiums, answering service invoices, software subscriptions, and the cost of calls nobody answers. Count both what you pay and what you lose when the phone rings into voicemail.**

Most owners only count the visible line item, such as the answering service bill. That understates the real number. The full cost has four buckets:

1. **Direct pay.** On-call stipends, call-out minimums, and overtime premiums for staff who cover nights and weekends. If overtime is part of your model, the math in [How to Calculate Overtime Cost for a Business: Formula and Examples](/blog/how-to-calculate-overtime-cost-for-a-business/) applies directly, because a 1.5x premium changes the per-hour cost fast.
2. **Vendor fees.** Answering service monthly minimums, per-minute or per-call charges, and overage fees.
3. **Software and setup.** Automation subscriptions, configuration time, and any one-time build cost.
4. **Missed opportunity cost.** Revenue from calls that go unanswered or unbooked. The formula in [How to Calculate the Cost of a Missed Call (With Formula)](/blog/how-to-calculate-the-cost-of-a-missed-call/) is the right tool for this bucket.

Add all four, and you have the true monthly cost of being reachable after 5 PM.

## The core formula: cost per covered hour

**Cost per covered hour is total monthly after-hours spend divided by total after-hours windows in the month. Cost per answered contact divides spend by actual calls handled. You need both numbers, because a cheap per-hour option can still be expensive per real conversation.**

The two formulas:

- **Cost per covered hour = Monthly after-hours cost / Covered hours per month**
- **Cost per answered contact = Monthly after-hours cost / After-hours contacts handled per month**

Count your covered hours first. If you close at 6 PM and open at 8 AM on weekdays, that is 14 hours per weekday, plus 48 weekend hours. A month with 22 weekdays and 8 weekend days gives you (22 x 14) + 48 = 356 uncovered hours. That is the window your coverage has to fill, and it is bigger than most owners expect.

Illustrative example: a three-truck plumbing company pays a $150 per week on-call stipend plus a two-hour call-out minimum at $60 per hour when the tech rolls. In a month with four weekends and eight call-outs, direct pay is roughly $600 in stipends plus $960 in call-out time, or $1,560. Divided by 356 covered hours, that is about $4.38 per covered hour. Divided by the 40 after-hours calls that month, it is $39 per answered contact. Both numbers matter, because the per-contact figure is what you compare against what a job is worth.

## Compare the three coverage models

**On-call staffing costs the most per hour but resolves real emergencies end to end. Answering services cost less and capture messages but rarely book or dispatch. Automation costs the least per contact and handles scheduling and triage, but it cannot turn a wrench. The right choice depends on call volume and job value.**

### On-call pay and overtime

The trade: high cost, full resolution. A tech who answers at 10 PM can actually fix the burst pipe. The cost drivers are the stipend, the call-out minimum, and the overtime premium on any hours past 40 in the week. If your after-hours techs also work full weekday schedules, the overtime math gets expensive quickly, so model it before you commit.

### Live answering service

Answering services typically charge a monthly base plus per-minute or per-call fees. The breakdown in [How Much Do Answering Services Charge? Pricing and Break-Even](/blog/how-much-do-answering-services-charge/) shows how quickly per-minute billing stacks up at moderate volume. These services are strong at message capture and basic triage scripts, but most cannot book into your calendar or dispatch a truck, so you still pay for callback time the next morning.

### Automation and AI coverage

An AI receptionist or chatbot answers instantly, books appointments, captures job details, and escalates true emergencies to an on-call phone. Subscription pricing is usually a flat monthly fee or a per-minute rate well below live agents, and [AI receptionist cost: what the three pricing models charge](/blog/ai-receptionist-cost/) walks through the pricing structures. The trade is judgment: automation handles the 80 percent of calls that are routine and escalates the rest.

| Model | Illustrative monthly cost | Cost per answered contact (40 calls) | Can it book or dispatch? |
|---|---|---|---|
| On-call staff | $1,560 | ~$39 | Yes, full resolution |
| Answering service | $400 to $700 | ~$10 to $18 | Usually message only |
| AI automation | $100 to $400 | ~$2.50 to $10 | Books and triages, escalates emergencies |
| No coverage | $0 direct | Missed-call losses instead | No |

All figures above are illustrative assumptions for comparison, not market quotes. Plug in your own vendor pricing and call volume.

## A worked example with illustrative numbers

**A plumbing company fields 40 after-hours calls a month, of which 10 are real emergencies worth $350 each and 30 are routine bookings worth $150 in average margin. Capturing everything is worth about $8,000 in monthly revenue, so even the most expensive coverage model can pay for itself if it converts calls into jobs.**

Here is the illustrative math for each option:

- **No coverage.** Assume 70 percent of callers do not leave a voicemail and call a competitor instead. That is 28 lost contacts. If even a third of those would have booked, the company loses roughly 9 to 10 jobs. At a blended $200 average margin per job, that is about $1,900 in lost margin per month.
- **On-call only.** $1,560 per month captures nearly everything, so net gain is roughly $1,900 minus $1,560, or about $340. Thin, because you are paying emergency-level cost for routine calls.
- **Answering service.** $550 per month captures messages for all 40 calls, but next-morning callbacks lose some routine callers to whoever answered first. Assume it saves 70 percent of the at-risk margin: about $1,330 gained minus $550, or $780 net.
- **Automation plus light on-call.** A $300 AI layer books the 30 routine calls instantly and escalates the 10 emergencies to a slimmed-down on-call rotation costing $700. Total $1,000, near-complete capture, net gain around $900.

The hybrid usually wins on paper because it matches cost to call type: cheap instant answers for routine work, expensive humans only for emergencies. ## When paying for after-hours coverage is not worth it

**After-hours coverage is not worth paying for when your after-hours volume is tiny and low value, such as fewer than five calls a month with margins under $100 each. In that case a clear voicemail greeting with a promised callback window and an online booking link costs almost nothing and loses very little.**

Be honest about three things before you spend. First, measure actual volume from your phone logs for at least two months; owners routinely overestimate how often the phone rings at night. Second, separate true emergencies from calls that can wait, because paying emergency rates for routine inquiries is the most common way this math goes wrong. Third, check seasonality. An HVAC company in July and the same company in October have completely different after-hours economics, so a flat annual contract may be wrong-sized half the year. If your measured lost margin is under a few hundred dollars a month, a better voicemail and a booking link beat any paid option.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the formula for after-hours coverage cost?

Divide your total monthly after-hours spend by the hours you need covered to get cost per covered hour, then divide by the number of contacts handled to get cost per answered contact. Total spend should include stipends, overtime premiums, vendor fees, software, and an estimate of missed-call losses.

### How many after-hours hours does a typical business need to cover?

A business open 8 AM to 6 PM on weekdays has about 14 uncovered hours per weekday plus full weekends, which is roughly 350 uncovered hours per month. Count your actual closed hours from your real schedule rather than assuming.

### Is an answering service cheaper than paying staff on call?

At low to moderate call volume, usually yes on direct cost, because you pay per minute instead of stipends and call-out minimums. The catch is that most answering services only take messages, so you still pay for morning callbacks and lose callers who will not wait. Compare cost per booked job, not cost per call.

### Can automation handle emergency calls?

Automation can triage emergencies by asking screening questions and immediately escalating to an on-call phone, but it cannot perform the physical work. The common setup is automation for routine booking and triage, with a slim on-call rotation reserved for genuine emergencies.

### How do I know if after-hours coverage is paying for itself?

Multiply the number of after-hours jobs booked each month by your average margin per job, then subtract your total coverage cost. If the result is positive across two or three months of phone-log data, coverage is paying for itself. If you do not track after-hours bookings yet, that tracking is your first step.
