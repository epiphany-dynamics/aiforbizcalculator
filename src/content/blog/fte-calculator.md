**An FTE calculator converts hours into staffing capacity: divide total monthly hours by 173.33, the average full-time month. Ten people working part time can equal four FTE. Before you claim automation ROI, convert the saved hours into FTE, then test whether that capacity is actually reclaimable.**

Most owners reach for an FTE number when a vendor says something like "saves you a full-time employee." That claim is checkable in about ten minutes with a task inventory and one division. The arithmetic below is capacity math, not compliance math, and not cost math.

## The formula an FTE calculator runs

**One FTE is one person working a standard full-time schedule. Divide total hours by the full-time hours for the same period: 40 in a week, 173.33 in a month, 2,080 in a year. The result is capacity, not people. Fractions are normal and expected.**

The base is fixed arithmetic, not an estimate. Forty hours a week times 52 weeks is 2,080 hours a year, and 2,080 divided by 12 is 173.33 hours a month. That is why a task consuming 87 hours a month is 0.5 FTE.

| Period | Standard full-time hours | FTE formula |
|---|---|---|
| Week | 40 | hours / 40 |
| Month | 173.33 | hours / 173.33 |
| Quarter | 520 | hours / 520 |
| Year | 2,080 | hours / 2,080 |

Two adjustments matter. If your standard week is 37.5 hours, your monthly base is 162.5 and every FTE figure rises by about 6 percent. And 173.33 counts scheduled hours, not productive ones: paid time off, breaks, meetings, and training all live inside it. Keep staffing capacity and cost separate. Cost belongs in a [fully loaded labor rate](/blog/fully-loaded-labor-rate/), where taxes, benefits, and overhead get added on top of wage.

## Headcount and FTE answer different questions

**Headcount counts bodies on the payroll. FTE counts the capacity those bodies deliver. A shop with two full-timers, three at 20 hours, and one at 10 hours has six on headcount and 3.75 FTE. Staffing plans, cost per unit of output, and savings claims all run on FTE.**

That example works out to 150 scheduled hours a week, and 150 divided by 40 is 3.75.

| Measure | What it counts | Where it belongs |
|---|---|---|
| Headcount | People on payroll | Org charts, benefits eligibility, scheduling coverage |
| FTE | Standard-schedule capacity | Staffing plans, cost per output, automation savings claims |

Mixed schedules are where owners overestimate themselves. Six names on the board feels like six people of coverage, but the work only ever gets 3.75 people worth of hours. The gap runs the other direction too: part-time capacity is harder to redeploy, because a 10 hour week rarely has a spare half-day sitting in it.

## Convert your task list into FTE before you price anything

**Automation vendors quote against tasks, so measure tasks. List each recurring job, multiply monthly volume by minutes per instance, sum the hours, then divide by 173.33. Most small-business front desks land under one FTE in total, which changes what a savings number can honestly mean.**

The two formulas:

- Monthly hours = (monthly volume x minutes per instance) / 60
- Task FTE = monthly hours / 173.33

Here is a worked example. Every number is illustrative, built for a hypothetical three-operatory dental practice with one front desk person and one part-time helper.

| Task | Monthly volume | Minutes each | Monthly hours | FTE |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Inbound call handling | 900 | 4 | 60.0 | 0.35 |
| Reminders and rescheduling | 400 | 3 | 20.0 | 0.12 |
| Insurance eligibility checks | 260 | 7 | 30.3 | 0.17 |
| Intake and data entry | 180 | 6 | 18.0 | 0.10 |
| **Total** | | | **128.3** | **0.74** |

The whole front-desk task bundle is 0.74 FTE. That single line kills a lot of sales math. If a vendor removes 60 percent of call handling and 80 percent of reminders, that is 36 hours plus 16 hours, or 52 hours a month. Divide by 173.33 and you get 0.30 FTE saved. Real, but it is not a salary, and it is split across two people who both still show up tomorrow.

## Test whether saved FTE is actually reclaimable

**Saved hours only become money if something changes on the payroll or on the revenue line. Run each block of freed capacity through five questions: does it cut overtime, cancel a planned hire, absorb growth, shift someone to revenue work, or vanish into slack? Only the first four count.**

1. **Does it eliminate paid overtime?** This is the cleanest conversion. Overtime hours are already priced and already on a pay stub, so removing them shows up next cycle.
2. **Does it cancel a hire already in the budget?** Value it at the fully loaded rate of the role you were about to post, starting on the month you would have started them.
3. **Does it absorb growth you already have?** If volume is climbing and the freed capacity lets the same team hold the line, the savings are the hire you now never make.
4. **Does it move someone to revenue-producing work?** Legitimate only if you can name the person, the new task, and how the revenue gets measured.
5. **None of the above?** It is slack. Put it in the model at zero and say so out loud.

Before you write a saved-FTE number into a business case, clear this checklist:

- [ ] The freed hours land mostly on one person, not sprinkled across four
- [ ] The block is large enough to restructure a role, roughly 0.25 FTE or more for that person (an illustrative working threshold, not a rule)
- [ ] A named manager owns the reassignment and knows what the person does instead
- [ ] A specific payroll or revenue line changes, on a specific date
- [ ] The hours are priced at fully loaded rate, not base wage

This is the step that separates a defensible estimate from a brochure number. ## When FTE math misleads you

**FTE treats hours as interchangeable, and often they are not. Coverage roles need a person present regardless of workload. Licensed tasks cannot move to whoever has spare minutes. And the ACA uses a different full-time definition entirely, so do not use this arithmetic for compliance filings.**

Three specific traps:

**The coverage floor.** If your phones must be answered from 8 to 5, you need someone there for 9 hours whether the work is 3 hours or 8. Automation below that floor buys calm, not payroll reduction.

**Credentialed work.** Freeing 0.4 FTE of admin time from a licensed clinician does not create 0.4 FTE of clinical capacity unless the schedule actually fills.

**Compliance FTE is a different number.** The ACA counts anyone at 30 or more hours a week as full time and converts part-time hours with a 120 hour monthly divisor for employer size determination. That test has nothing to do with the 173.33 capacity math here. Ask your accountant for that one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is the monthly figure 173.33 hours?

It comes from a full year, not a four-week month. Forty hours a week times 52 weeks equals 2,080 hours a year, and 2,080 divided by 12 months equals 173.33. Using 160 hours (four 40 hour weeks) understates the month and inflates every FTE result by about 8 percent.

### Is 0.4 FTE saved the same as saving 40 percent of a salary?

Only if the payroll actually changes. Hours saved become cash when they cut overtime, cancel a hire, absorb growth, or shift someone onto revenue work. If none of those happen, the hours turn into slack and the correct value in your model is zero.

### Should I use 2,080 hours or actual paid hours?

Use 2,080 for capacity planning, since it is the standard schedule everyone compares against. Use actual paid hours when you are calculating cost per hour, because that is where holidays, sick time, and unpaid gaps change the denominator and therefore the true rate.

### What if my team works 37.5 hour weeks?

Substitute your own base. A 37.5 hour week gives 1,950 annual hours and 162.5 monthly hours. Apply the same division. Keep the base consistent across every task and every scenario, or your before and after comparison will not mean anything.

### How do I estimate minutes per task without time tracking?

Have the person doing the work log a representative week, or time five real instances and take the median. Then round up. Estimates from memory tend to run low on the small interruptions, which are usually the hours automation removes first.
