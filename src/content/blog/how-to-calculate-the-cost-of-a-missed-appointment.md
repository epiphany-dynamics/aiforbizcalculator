**The cost of a missed appointment equals lost slot value plus idle labor cost: (average ticket price) plus (idle minutes divided by 60, multiplied by the staff member's fully loaded hourly rate). This gives an honest floor on the loss without assuming any recovery rate for rebooked or backfilled slots.**

## What the missed appointment actually costs

**A missed appointment costs more than the ticket price you lost. It also costs the wages you paid a provider, technician, or stylist to stay ready for a client who never showed. Add both pieces together and you get the real cost, not just the revenue number most owners quote when they talk about no-shows.**

Most cost estimates for missed appointments stop at "we lost a $90 haircut" or "we lost a $150 therapy session." That is the slot value, and it is real. But the staff member assigned to that slot did not disappear. They were paid to be there, whether or not the client walked in. That paid, unproductive time is idle labor cost, and ignoring it understates the true damage of every no-show.

## The slot value plus idle labor formula

**Missed appointment cost equals slot value plus idle labor cost. Slot value is the average revenue that appointment type would have generated. Idle labor cost is the idle minutes divided by 60, multiplied by the fully loaded hourly rate of the staff member who was scheduled for that slot. Add the two for a per-incident total.**

| Component | What it measures | How to calculate |
|---|---|---|
| Slot value | Revenue lost from the service not delivered | Average ticket price for that appointment type |
| Idle labor cost | Wages paid for unproductive staff time | (Idle minutes / 60) x fully loaded hourly rate |
| Missed appointment cost | Total cost of one no-show | Slot value + idle labor cost |

If you have not calculated a fully loaded hourly rate before, including payroll taxes, benefits, and overhead, that number matters here. A quick way to build one is covered in [fully loaded labor rate: how to calculate yours](/blog/fully-loaded-labor-rate/). Using a raw wage instead of the fully loaded rate will understate your real cost.

## Illustrative example: a single missed haircut appointment

**Assume a stylist's average ticket is $65, the appointment is 45 minutes, and the stylist's fully loaded hourly rate is $28. Idle labor cost is 45/60 x $28, or $21. Total missed appointment cost is $65 plus $21, which equals $86 for that one no-show, before any assumption about rebooking.**

This is an illustrative example with assumed numbers, not a benchmark. Walk the math:

1. Slot value: $65 (average ticket for a cut and style)
2. Idle time: 45 minutes with no other client in that chair
3. Idle labor cost: 45/60 x $28 = $21
4. Total cost: $65 + $21 = $86

Notice that the idle labor piece adds roughly a third on top of the ticket price. That is the part most salons and clinics never add to their internal math, and it is the gap between "we lost a sale" and "we lost the sale and paid someone to wait for it."

## Illustrative example: three no-shows in one clinic day

**Assume a physical therapy clinic bills $120 per visit, sessions run 50 minutes, and the therapist's fully loaded rate is $52 per hour. Idle labor cost per no-show is $43.33. Total cost per missed visit is $163.33. Three no-shows in one day cost the clinic roughly $490, not the $360 a revenue-only estimate would show.**

Run the same four steps for one visit:

1. Slot value: $120
2. Idle time: 50 minutes
3. Idle labor cost: 50/60 x $52 = $43.33
4. Total per visit: $120 + $43.33 = $163.33

Multiply by three no-shows: $163.33 x 3 = $489.99, rounded to $490. A clinic that only tracks lost billing would report $360 in lost revenue for the day and miss the extra $130 in paid, idle therapist time. ## When this calculation does not apply

**This formula assumes a scheduled slot with a specific staff member sitting idle. It breaks down for walk-in businesses, double-booked schedules where a backup client fills the gap, and shops that deliberately overbook to absorb no-shows. In those cases, idle labor cost may be zero or much smaller than the formula suggests.**

If your front desk double-books slots as a policy, or if a walk-in immediately fills a canceled appointment, the idle labor term should be reduced or dropped for that slot. Do not apply the full formula to every gap on the calendar without checking whether staff time was actually wasted. The formula is meant to measure real, paid, unproductive time, not every empty square on a schedule.

## Turning the number into a decision

**Once you have a per-appointment cost, multiply it by your monthly no-show count to get a monthly loss figure. Compare that figure to the cost of fixes like deposits, reminder systems, or waitlist automation. If the fix costs less than the monthly loss, it is worth testing before you assume any specific recovery rate.**

A simple decision checklist:

- Calculate your average slot value by appointment type
- Calculate your fully loaded hourly rate for each staff role
- Multiply idle minutes by that rate for each no-show
- Add slot value and idle labor cost for a per-incident total
- Multiply by your actual monthly no-show count
- Compare that monthly total to the cost of a fix, without assuming the fix recovers 100 percent of the loss

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does this formula assume a recovery rate for missed slots?

No. This formula only measures the cost of one missed appointment: slot value plus idle labor. It does not assume any percentage of that loss gets recovered through rebooking, waitlists, or same-day fill-ins. If you want to model recovery, calculate it separately and subtract it from the total, using your own actual rebooking data rather than an assumed percentage.

### What counts as idle labor time for a missed appointment?

Idle labor time is the portion of the scheduled appointment where the assigned staff member had no other billable work to do. If a stylist, therapist, or technician could not immediately serve another client during that window, the full appointment length counts as idle time for this calculation.

### How do I find my fully loaded hourly rate?

Start with the staff member's base wage, then add payroll taxes, benefits, and a share of fixed overhead like rent and equipment, divided by their working hours. [Fully loaded labor rate: how to calculate yours](/blog/fully-loaded-labor-rate/) walks through the full calculation with examples.

### Is a missed appointment more expensive than a missed phone call?

It depends on the ticket size and the idle time involved. A missed appointment usually ties up a specific staff member for the full scheduled length, while a missed call may cost only a few minutes of front desk time plus the value of the lost booking. Compare both using [how to calculate the cost of a missed call (with formula)](/blog/how-to-calculate-the-cost-of-a-missed-call/) to see which gap is costing your business more.

### Should I use average ticket price or the specific client's expected spend?

Average ticket price by appointment type is simpler and works well for most small businesses. If a specific no-show client had a known, larger booking, such as a package or a high-margin service, use that actual figure instead for a more accurate single-incident calculation.
