**The cost of a missed call is the revenue you would have earned if someone answered, multiplied by your margin. This guide gives you the exact formula, the four inputs you need from your own records, a worked example with illustrative numbers, and a way to turn the result into a staffing or automation decision.**

## The core formula

**The monthly cost of missed calls equals your missed call count multiplied by the share of callers who never call back, multiplied by your close rate, multiplied by average job value, multiplied by gross margin. Each input narrows the number so you stop counting calls that were never going to become paying work.**

Written out:

**Monthly missed call cost = Missed calls × (1 − callback rate) × close rate × average job value × gross margin**

Here is what each input means in practice:

- **Missed calls.** Calls that rang out, hit voicemail without a message, or arrived when nobody was working.
- **Callback rate.** The share of missed callers who try again and reach you. If 30% call back, your no-callback rate is 70%, and only that 70% is truly at risk.
- **Close rate.** The share of reached callers who book a job, appointment, or order.
- **Average job value.** Your typical ticket, not your best one.
- **Gross margin.** Revenue minus direct cost of delivering the work. Margin, not revenue, is the money you actually lose.

Two inputs (callback rate and close rate) are probabilities. Two (job value and margin) are dollars. Multiply them all and you get expected margin lost per month.

## Step one: count the calls you actually miss

**Most owners guess their missed call count and guess low. Pull 30 days of records from your phone carrier, VoIP dashboard, or call log app. Count calls that rang out, voicemails with no message, and calls outside staffed hours. That total, not your hunch, is the starting input.**

Three places the data usually hides:

1. **Carrier or VoIP reports.** Most business phone systems show answered versus unanswered calls per day. Export one month.
2. **Voicemail logs.** A voicemail with no message is a missed call. A voicemail with a message is a partial save, but only if you returned it quickly.
3. **After-hours traffic.** If you close at 5 pm and your call log shows 15 calls between 5 pm and 9 am, those are missed by definition unless someone answers.

Define "missed" once and stick to it. A reasonable definition: any inbound call that did not reach a person or an automated system capable of booking within one ring cycle of business hours, plus every call outside business hours.

## Step two: turn missed calls into lost revenue

**Not every missed call is a lost sale. Some callers dial a competitor within minutes, and others try again later. Callers to local service businesses frequently buy from whoever responds first, so assume a meaningful share never returns, then apply your own close rate and average ticket to that share only.**

This is where honesty matters most. Two illustrative scenarios:

- **Emergency plumber.** A caller with a burst pipe will not leave a voicemail and wait. A no-callback rate of 80% to 90% is a reasonable planning assumption.
- **Landscaper quoting a spring cleanup.** The caller is gathering three quotes over a week. A no-callback rate of 30% to 50% is more realistic because they will try you again.

Your close rate should come from your own numbers: booked jobs divided by inbound inquiries you actually spoke to. If you do not track it, start tracking this month, because every downstream dollar figure depends on it.

Missed calls also distort your marketing math. If you pay for leads and then miss the call, your effective cost per booked lead climbs. ## A worked example with illustrative numbers

**Here is a fully worked example using hypothetical numbers for an imaginary plumbing company. Adjust every input to match your own records. The point is the shape of the math: a modest monthly miss count compounds into a five-figure annual margin leak once you multiply it by twelve months.**

| Input | Illustrative value | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Missed calls per month | 40 | Phone system report |
| No-callback rate | 70% | Planning assumption for urgent work |
| Close rate on reached callers | 25% | Booking records |
| Average job value | $450 | Last 90 days of invoices |
| Gross margin | 40% | Profit and loss statement |

The math:

- Lost opportunities: 40 × 0.70 = 28 callers gone for good
- Lost jobs: 28 × 0.25 = 7 jobs per month
- Lost revenue: 7 × $450 = $3,150 per month
- **Lost margin: $3,150 × 0.40 = $1,260 per month, or $15,120 per year**

That $1,260 is the number that matters. It is the monthly budget ceiling for any fix, whether that is an answering service, an AI receptionist, or a schedule change.

## What the simple formula misses

**The formula counts only the first job. It ignores repeat business, referrals, and reviews from customers you never won. That understates lifetime damage, but it also risks double-counting if you bolt on lifetime value without adjusting for customers who would have churned anyway. Pick one lens and stay consistent.**

A customer who would have booked four jobs over three years is worth more than one $450 ticket. The honest limitation: this math assumes missed calls are real buyers. If a third of your unanswered calls are spam, robocalls, or wrong numbers, shrink your missed call count before running the formula. Do not use an inflated miss count to justify a purchase you would not make with clean data. ## What to do with the number

**Once you have a monthly cost, compare it against the monthly price of fixing the problem. If missed calls cost you $1,260 in margin and a fix costs $300, the payback is immediate. If the fix costs more than the loss, you either counted wrong or the honest answer is to leave the phone as is.**

Your main options, in rough order of cost:

1. **Change coverage.** Stagger shifts so someone answers during your peak miss windows. Cheapest if you already have staff.
2. **Answering service or virtual receptionist.** Humans answer for a per-minute or per-call fee.
3. **AI receptionist or automated callback.** Software answers, books, or texts the caller back instantly.
4. **Full call handling platform.** Only worth it at higher volume, and the [real first-year total in call center software pricing](/blog/call-center-software-pricing/) is usually higher than the sticker price once setup and seats are counted.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What callback rate should I assume?

Use your own data if you have it: check how many missed callers appear again in your call log within 48 hours. Without data, pick a conservative assumption based on urgency. Emergency services can justify 80% to 90% never returning. Quote-shopping businesses should assume 30% to 50%. Label the number as an assumption in your spreadsheet so you can revise it.

### Do after-hours calls count as missed calls?

Yes, if a real buyer called and nobody could respond. The exception is when your business genuinely gets no after-hours demand. Check your logs for a month before deciding. Many service businesses find a large share of weekly calls arrive before 9 am, after 5 pm, or on weekends.

### Should I use revenue or margin in the formula?

Margin. Lost revenue overstates the damage because you also avoided the cost of delivering the work. A $450 job at 40% margin costs you $180, not $450. Use revenue only if you are comparing against a fix priced as a share of revenue, and even then, margin is the safer planning number.

### How many missed calls justify paying for a fix?

Work backward from the fix's price. If a solution costs $300 per month and your average job contributes $180 in margin, it needs to recover about two jobs a month to break even. At a 25% close rate and 70% no-callback rate, that is roughly 12 missed calls a month. Below that, free fixes like call forwarding may be enough.

### What if most of my missed calls are spam?

Filter first, calculate second. Review a sample of unanswered calls and mark which were real prospects. If half were robocalls, cut your missed call count in half before running the formula. A fix bought against inflated numbers will never show the payback the spreadsheet promised.
