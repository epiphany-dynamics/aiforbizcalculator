**Average customer acquisition cost varies widely by industry and channel, typically landing between $50 and $500 for small businesses, but the average matters less than your gap: (your CAC minus the benchmark) x customers acquired per month equals the dollars you are overpaying or saving every single month.**

## What counts as customer acquisition cost

**Customer acquisition cost (CAC) is total sales and marketing spend divided by new customers gained in the same period. Leaving out ad spend, tool subscriptions, commissions, or a founder's selling hours is the single most common reason a business's CAC looks artificially low.**

The formula:

CAC = (Marketing spend + Sales spend) / Number of new customers acquired

For a period, add up:

- Paid ad spend (Google, Meta, LinkedIn, etc.)
- Marketing tool and software subscriptions
- Sales team wages or commissions tied to new-customer deals
- Content, SEO, and agency fees
- A reasonable hourly value for owner time spent selling, if that time is substantial

**Illustrative example:** A home services company spends $3,000 on ads, $400 on a CRM, and pays a part-time closer $1,600 in commissions in one month. They close 20 new customers. CAC = $5,000 / 20 = $250 per customer.

## Average CAC by channel and business type

**There is no single "correct" average CAC because it depends on deal size, industry, and channel mix. The figures below are illustrative ranges assembled from commonly cited small-business benchmarks, meant to give you a rough starting point, not an exact target to hit.**

| Business type | Typical illustrative CAC range | Common acquisition channels |
|---|---|---|
| Local service business (HVAC, dental, salon) | $50 to $200 | Local search, referrals, Google Ads |
| B2B professional services | $200 to $800 | Outbound sales, LinkedIn, referrals |
| E-commerce (low ticket) | $15 to $45 | Paid social, marketplace ads |
| SaaS (small business tier) | $200 to $600 | Content, paid search, trials |
| High-ticket home services (roofing, solar) | $300 to $900 | Direct mail, paid search, canvassing |

Treat these as a sanity check, not gospel. A dental practice paying $150 to acquire a patient worth $2,000 in lifetime value is in a very different position than a low-ticket e-commerce store paying $150 for a $60 order.

## How to calculate your CAC gap against the average

**The CAC gap is the dollar difference between your actual CAC and the benchmark for your business type, multiplied by your monthly customer volume. This turns an abstract percentage into a real budget number you can act on, showing exactly how much extra you are spending, or saving, compared to peers.**

The formula:

CAC gap ($) = (Your CAC - Benchmark CAC) x New customers per month

**Illustrative example:** The home services company above has a CAC of $250. If the benchmark range for their category tops out around $200, they are $50 over on every customer. At 20 new customers a month, that is a $1,000 monthly gap, or $12,000 a year, above what a similar business "should" be paying.

Run the same math in the other direction. If your CAC comes in under the benchmark, that gap is money you are effectively saving versus a typical competitor, and it is worth understanding why so you can protect it as you scale.

## What a high CAC gap actually costs you

**A CAC gap does not just erode margin on paper. It compounds every month you keep spending at the same acquisition mix, and it quietly shrinks the return on any marketing or sales investment layered on top of it, including automation or AI tools meant to improve efficiency.**

Three places the gap shows up:

1. **Margin per customer.** If your average customer is worth $600 in gross profit and your CAC is $50 over benchmark, that single overage eats over 8% of that customer's contribution before you have delivered anything.
2. **Payback period.** A higher CAC pushes out the point where a customer becomes profitable, which matters most for subscription or repeat-service businesses with thin monthly margins.
3. **Investment decisions.** Owners evaluating a new tool, hire, or automation often compare the cost against expected ROI. If your baseline CAC is inflated, you will underestimate how much that new investment needs to save just to break even. ## How to close the gap without cutting corners

**Closing a CAC gap usually comes from fixing conversion leaks or channel mix, not from slashing marketing spend outright, which often just lowers volume alongside cost. Start with the channel producing the most customers, since a small efficiency gain there moves the average faster than a fix on a minor channel.**

A simple decision checklist:

- [ ] List every channel and its individual CAC, not just the blended average
- [ ] Identify which channel is furthest above benchmark
- [ ] Check whether the issue is spend (too many wasted clicks) or conversion (leads not closing)
- [ ] Test one change at a time: offer, landing page, follow-up speed, or lead qualification
- [ ] Recalculate CAC after 30 days before making a second change
- [ ] Compare the new gap in dollars, not just percentage, to confirm real savings

Missed calls and slow follow-up are a common, underweighted driver of high CAC because paid leads that never get answered still count as spend with no customer attached. ## When comparing to averages is misleading

**Benchmarks published across the industry mix wildly different business sizes, deal values, and definitions of "customer," so a raw average can send you chasing the wrong number. Use benchmarks as a directional gut check, then anchor your real decisions to your own historical CAC trend over time.**

A newer business with a small sample size (say, five customers in a month) will see CAC swing wildly month to month regardless of what it does. In that case, look at a rolling three-month average instead of comparing a single month against an industry figure. Similarly, a business with an unusually high average order value or lifetime value can rationally sustain a CAC well above the "average" and still be profitable, so do not chase a lower number for its own sake if the unit economics already work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a good customer acquisition cost for a small business?

There is no universal good number. A useful rule of thumb is that CAC should be no more than one-third of a customer's lifetime value, though businesses with fast repeat purchases or long contracts can sometimes sustain a higher ratio. Compare your CAC to your own margin structure before comparing it to an industry average.

### How often should I recalculate my CAC?

Monthly is reasonable for most small businesses, though if your customer volume is low, a rolling three-month average smooths out noise from any single slow or unusually strong month.

### Does customer acquisition cost include existing customer retention spend?

No. CAC should only include spend tied to acquiring new customers. Retention, upsell, and renewal costs belong in a separate metric, often called customer retention cost, so blending them will understate your true acquisition cost.

### Why is my CAC higher than the industry average even though sales are growing?

Growing sales alongside a high CAC often means you are winning on volume but leaking margin on each deal. Check whether growth is coming from a channel with rising costs, like paid search bids climbing, before assuming growth alone means the CAC is fine.

### Can automation actually lower customer acquisition cost?

It can, mainly by improving conversion on leads you are already paying for, such as faster call answering or follow-up, rather than by reducing ad spend itself.
