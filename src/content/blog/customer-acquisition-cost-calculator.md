**Customer acquisition cost (CAC) equals total marketing spend plus total sales spend for a period, divided by the number of new customers acquired in that same period. This article gives you the exact formula, a worked example, what costs belong in the numerator, and when CAC alone will mislead you.**

## The CAC formula for small businesses

**CAC = (total marketing spend + total sales spend) / number of new customers acquired in the period. Use the same time window for spend and new customers, and use fully loaded costs, not just ad spend, so the number reflects what it actually costs to win one customer.**

The formula looks simple, and it is, but most small businesses get the inputs wrong in two ways. First, they only count ad spend and skip payroll for the people running sales and marketing. Second, they mismatch the time window, counting spend from one month against customers who signed in another. Fix both and CAC becomes a genuinely useful number instead of a vanity metric.

CAC is not the same as marketing spend per lead, and it is not the same as cost per click. It is the cost per *closed, paying customer*, which is the number that ties directly to your margins.

## How to calculate CAC step by step (with worked example)

**Pull total spend for a fixed period, add sales and marketing together, then divide by new customers won in that period. Below is an illustrative example for a home services company running a one-month test, showing every input so you can substitute your own numbers directly.**

Illustrative example (hypothetical numbers for a local HVAC company, one calendar month):

1. Marketing spend: $4,200 (Google ads, social ads, a marketing coordinator's salary share)
2. Sales spend: $2,800 (a part-time sales rep's salary share, CRM software, phone system)
3. Total spend: $7,000
4. New customers acquired: 20
5. CAC = $7,000 / 20 = **$350 per new customer**

If that same company closes new customers at an average first-job value of $900, a $350 CAC still leaves healthy margin. If the average first job is $400, that same CAC barely covers the cost of winning the work before you have delivered anything. The formula tells you the cost; your own margins tell you whether that cost is acceptable.

Run the same calculation every month or every quarter using a consistent window, and track it as a trend line rather than a single snapshot. One month of CAC tells you little. Six months of CAC tells you whether a channel or a hire is actually paying off.

## What counts as marketing and sales spend

**Include every dollar spent trying to win a new customer, not just ad budgets. That means paid media, content and SEO costs, marketing software subscriptions, agency or freelancer fees, and the salaried or commissioned cost of anyone whose job is generating or closing new business.**

A practical checklist for building your CAC numerator:

- Paid advertising (search, social, display, local listings boosts)
- Marketing software and tools (email platform, landing page builder, analytics)
- Content, SEO, or agency fees tied to acquisition
- Salaries or salary share for marketing staff
- Salaries, commissions, or salary share for sales staff
- Sales tools (CRM, dialer, proposal software)
- Events, sponsorships, or local marketing (yard signs, truck wraps, sponsorships) if their purpose is new-customer acquisition

Leave out costs tied to retaining or servicing existing customers, such as customer success salaries or loyalty program costs. Those belong in a retention cost calculation, not CAC.

## CAC by channel: compare where your customers come from

**Calculating one blended CAC hides which channels are actually working. Break spend and new customers out by source, ideally into a simple table, so you can compare cost per customer across channels and shift budget toward the ones that are actually profitable.**

Illustrative example, same HVAC company, one month, split by channel:

| Channel | Spend | New customers | CAC |
|---|---|---|---|
| Google Ads | $2,000 | 8 | $250 |
| Referrals | $300 (referral incentive) | 6 | $50 |
| Social ads | $1,900 | 4 | $475 |
| Outbound sales calls | $2,800 | 2 | $1,400 |
| **Blended total** | **$7,000** | **20** | **$350** |

The blended $350 CAC from the earlier example looks fine on its own, but this breakdown shows referrals are far cheaper than outbound calling, and social ads are underperforming Google Ads by nearly double. A business that only tracks the blended number would never see that outbound sales calls are the weak link. This is the actual value of the CAC formula: it is only useful once you can compare channels against each other, not just against a single average.

## What counts as a good CAC (and the CAC to LTV check)

**CAC only means something next to customer lifetime value. A commonly used rule of thumb is that lifetime value should be at least three times CAC, though the right ratio depends on your margins, payback period, and how much cash you have to front acquisition costs before revenue arrives.**

To check your ratio: estimate average customer lifetime value (average job or contract value multiplied by expected repeat purchases or contract length), then divide by CAC. A 3:1 ratio or higher generally means you have room to keep investing in growth. A ratio near 1:1 means you are roughly breaking even on acquisition, which is risky if a customer churns early or a job falls through.

Also check payback period: how many months of revenue does it take to recover CAC? A business with a $350 CAC and a $900 average job recovers that cost on the first sale. A subscription business with a $350 CAC and $40/month recurring revenue needs nearly 9 months of retention just to break even, which changes how aggressively you can spend on acquisition.

## When CAC alone can mislead you

**CAC is a lagging, blended average that hides timing, channel mix, and customer quality. Do not use it as your only growth metric, especially for seasonal businesses, long sales cycles, or when comparing a brand-new channel against an established one with years of compounding referrals behind it.**

A few honest limits to keep in mind: CAC calculated over a single slow month will look artificially high even if your process hasn't changed. A channel that is brand new will always show a worse CAC than a five-year-old referral program, because referrals compound and new channels do not. And CAC says nothing about customer quality: a $50 CAC customer who cancels in a month can cost you more than a $400 CAC customer who stays for years. Always read CAC next to retention and lifetime value, not by itself.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a good customer acquisition cost for a small business?

There is no universal number; it depends entirely on your average customer value and margins. A useful check is whether lifetime value is at least three times CAC, and whether you can recover CAC within a payback period your cash flow can tolerate, commonly a few months for service businesses and longer for subscription models.

### Does CAC include employee salaries?

Yes. Fully loaded CAC includes the salary or commission of anyone whose job is generating leads or closing new business, not just paid ad spend. Leaving salaries out understates your true cost per customer and can make an unprofitable channel look profitable.

### How often should I calculate CAC?

Monthly for fast-moving channels like paid ads, and quarterly for slower channels like referrals or SEO where results lag the spend. Track the trend over time rather than judging a single month, since seasonality and one-off campaigns can swing a single period's number significantly.

### What's the difference between CAC and cost per lead?

Cost per lead only measures spend divided by leads generated, regardless of whether those leads ever became paying customers. CAC divides spend by actual new customers, which accounts for your close rate. A channel can have a low cost per lead and a high CAC if its leads rarely convert.

### Can automation or AI tools lower my CAC?

They can, mainly by increasing your close rate on leads you're already paying for, such as faster response times or fewer missed calls, rather than by cutting ad spend directly. [How to use an AI automation ROI calculator (with a worked example)](/blog/ai-automation-roi-calculator) walks through modeling that kind of impact with real inputs before you commit budget.

### Should CAC include the cost of retaining existing customers?

No. CAC measures the cost of acquiring new customers only. Retention costs, such as customer success salaries or loyalty programs, belong in a separate retention or customer service cost calculation, since mixing the two understates true acquisition cost and overstates retention cost.
