**A gross profit calculator subtracts your direct costs from revenue to show what each sale actually contributes before overhead. This page gives you the formula, a worked example with clearly labeled illustrative numbers, and a decision framework for which gross profit assumptions are honest enough to put in an automation business case.**

## The gross profit formula, without accounting jargon

**Gross profit is revenue minus the direct cost of delivering what you sold: materials, direct labor, and per-unit fulfillment. It ignores rent, office salaries, and marketing. The formula is one subtraction, but the hard part is deciding which costs are truly direct for your specific business, and that decision changes the answer.**

The formula:

**Gross profit = Revenue - Cost of goods sold (COGS)**

For a product business, COGS means materials, inbound freight, packaging, and direct production labor. For a service business, the equivalent is the cost of delivering the service: technician or consultant hours billed to the job, subcontractor fees, and materials consumed on that job.

Here is a worked example with illustrative numbers for a small services firm:

| Line item | Illustrative monthly amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $40,000 |
| Direct delivery labor | $12,000 |
| Materials and subcontractors | $6,000 |
| Total direct cost (COGS) | $18,000 |
| **Gross profit** | **$22,000** |
| Gross margin | 55% |

These numbers are illustrative, not benchmarks. Your figures come from your own invoices and timesheets.

The subtraction is the easy part. The classification is the work. Office rent, the owner's salary, software subscriptions, and marketing spend do not belong in COGS because they do not rise or fall with each unit sold. If a cost stays the same whether you sell ten jobs or fifty, it is overhead, and it belongs below the gross profit line.

## Gross profit, gross margin, and net profit are not the same number

**Gross profit is a dollar amount, gross margin is that amount divided by revenue as a percentage, and net profit is what remains after every operating cost. Owners mix these up constantly, and vendors exploit the confusion by quoting a margin improvement as if it were cash profit in the bank.**

| Metric | Formula | What it tells you | Common misuse |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gross profit | Revenue - COGS | Dollars left to cover overhead | Treated as take-home profit |
| Gross margin | Gross profit / Revenue | Pricing power and delivery efficiency | Quoted without the dollar base |
| Net profit | Revenue - all costs | What the business actually keeps | Confused with gross profit in pitches |

A margin percentage without the dollar base hides scale. Moving from 50% to 55% margin on $40,000 of revenue is worth $2,000 a month. The same five-point move on $400,000 is worth $20,000. Always convert percentages back to dollars before you make a decision with them.

This matters most when someone pitches you an automation tool. A claim like "improves margin by 8 points" means nothing until you know whose revenue base, whose definition of direct cost, and whether the tool's own subscription fee was counted as a cost.

## Which gross profit assumptions belong in an automation business case

**An automation business case should only include gross profit assumptions you can trace to an invoice, a timesheet, or a price list. Time saved, errors avoided, and capacity freed must each map to a real direct cost line. Anything you cannot trace stays out of the base case and goes into an upside column instead.**

Use this five-step test before any number enters your business case:

1. **Start from a traced cost.** Find the direct cost line the automation touches, such as delivery labor hours per job, and pull its real current value from payroll or job costing records.
2. **Convert time saved into dollars honestly.** Multiply hours saved by the actual wage or contractor rate of the person doing the work, not a blended or aspirational rate.
3. **Treat freed capacity as soft value until it converts.** An hour saved is only gross profit if you stop paying for that hour or fill it with billable work. Otherwise it is potential, and it belongs in the upside column.
4. **Keep error reduction separate.** Fewer remakes or refunds improve gross profit, but estimate them from your last six months of actual rework records, not from a vendor case study.
5. **Run three scenarios.** Build conservative, base, and optimistic versions. If the decision only works in the optimistic case, the honest answer is no or not yet.

## A worked example with illustrative numbers

**A worked example makes the discipline concrete. Using illustrative figures, a services firm with $40,000 monthly revenue and $18,000 direct delivery cost has $22,000 in gross profit. Automation that cuts direct cost by $3,000 adds that amount to gross profit, but that addition is not the same thing as ROI.**

Continuing the example from above: the firm automates part of its job intake and scheduling, which removes 75 hours a month of $40-per-hour delivery labor from direct cost. That is $3,000 a month traced to a real timesheet line.

| Line item | Before automation | After automation |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $40,000 | $40,000 |
| Direct delivery labor | $12,000 | $9,000 |
| Materials and subcontractors | $6,000 | $6,000 |
| Gross profit | $22,000 | $25,000 |
| Gross margin | 55% | 62.5% |

All figures remain illustrative. Now the part most pitches skip: the $3,000 monthly gross profit gain is not ROI. If the automation costs $9,000 to set up and $500 a month to run, the net monthly gain is $2,500, and the setup cost takes roughly four months to recover. That recovery figure is the real decision number, and the [payback period calculator: the automation version of the math](/blog/payback-period-calculator/) walks through exactly that calculation.

Gross profit tells you whether the automation improves the unit economics of each job. Payback and net profit tell you whether the investment is worth making at all. You need both, in that order.

## When gross profit is the wrong number to use

**Gross profit misleads when direct costs are tiny relative to price, when you compare projects with different overhead structures, or when a vendor treats a margin gain as cash in hand. In those situations you need contribution margin, net profit, or a payback calculation instead of a gross figure.**

Three cases where you should reach for a different metric:

- **Businesses with near-zero direct costs.** A bookkeeping firm that sells a software subscription alongside its service may show a 90% gross margin on that line. Gross profit adds no information there; net profit after support and churn costs is the honest figure.
- **Retention and pricing decisions.** Choosing between keeping a customer and acquiring a new one should run on margin-weighted lifetime value, not gross profit per transaction. The [customer lifetime value calculator: margin, not revenue](/blog/customer-lifetime-value-calculator/) explains why revenue-based LTV overstates what a retained customer is worth.
- **Cross-project comparisons.** Two automation projects can show identical gross profit gains while one adds overhead (a new admin hire to manage the tool) and the other does not. Only net profit separates them.

The honest limitation of any gross profit calculator, including this framework, is that it cannot see below the gross line. A project can improve gross profit and still lose money overall. Treat gross profit as the first filter, never the final verdict.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is gross profit the same as ROI?

No. Gross profit is revenue minus direct delivery cost. ROI compares a net gain against the full cost of an investment, including setup, subscriptions, and ongoing labor. An automation can raise gross profit every month and still produce negative ROI if its total cost exceeds the gain.

### What counts as cost of goods sold for a service business?

The direct cost of delivering the service: hours billed to the job at actual wage rates, subcontractor fees, and materials consumed on that job. Rent, marketing, owner salary, and admin software are overhead and stay below the gross profit line because they do not scale with each job sold.

### What is a good gross margin for a small business?

There is no universal number, and any single benchmark quoted without an industry context should be treated with suspicion. Margins vary widely between product, service, and mixed businesses. The useful comparison is your own margin over time and against businesses with the same delivery model as yours.

### Can automation improve gross profit without raising prices?

Yes, by reducing the direct cost side of the formula. Cutting delivery labor hours, subcontractor spend, or material waste per job raises gross profit at the same price and volume. Whether that improvement justifies the automation's total cost is a separate question that payback math answers.

### Should I use revenue or gross profit when sizing an automation investment?

Use gross profit. An automation that costs $12,000 a year needs to generate at least that much in traced gross profit gains or other measurable value to break even. Sizing the investment against revenue ignores the direct costs that revenue still has to cover.
