**A virtual assistant's real cost is the quoted hourly rate plus your management time, turnover, and ramp. Software's real cost is the subscription plus setup and the exceptions it hands back. Price both as annual loaded totals for the same defined task, then compare at your actual volume. Below a few hours a week, the human usually wins.**

## What you are actually comparing

**A virtual assistant is general labor priced by the hour and pointed at whatever you need this week. Automation is one narrow task priced by the month and unchanged until you change it. Comparing an hourly rate to a subscription is a category error. Convert both to annual loaded cost for the same defined slice of work.**

The comparison only becomes answerable once you name the slice. "Help with admin" cannot be priced against anything. "Answer inbound booking requests between 8am and 6pm" can. So can "send appointment reminders at 48 hours and 2 hours," or "chase unpaid invoices at day 7, 14, and 30."

Write the slice down in one sentence. Then estimate how many hours per week it consumes today. That single number decides most of what follows, and it is the number owners most often guess wrong. Track it for a week before you price anything.

## The loaded cost of a virtual assistant

**The quoted rate covers the VA's hours, not yours. Add the time you spend assigning, reviewing, and correcting work, plus the cost of replacing someone once a year. Loaded cost commonly lands well above the quote, and it is the only number worth comparing against software.**

The formula:

```
Loaded annual VA cost =
    (hourly rate x hours per week x 50 weeks)
  + (your management hours per week x your hourly value x 50)
  + (turnover events per year x rehire and retrain cost)
  + platform, tool, and payment fees
```

Illustrative example, using assumed numbers to show the mechanics rather than a market benchmark. Say you hire at $22 per hour for 15 hours a week, you value your own time at $75 per hour, and you spend 2 hours a week managing the role.

| Line item | Illustrative annual cost |
|---|---|
| Base hours (22 x 15 x 50) | $16,500 |
| Your management time (2 x 75 x 50) | $7,500 |
| One turnover event (hiring plus reduced output during ramp) | $1,800 |
| Platform fees and tool seats | $600 |
| **Loaded total** | **$26,400** |

That is 750 covered hours at $35.20 per hour, against a $22 quote. The gap is not waste. It is the price of flexibility, and a good VA earns it by absorbing exceptions you never see. But if you compare $22 against a subscription, you are comparing the wrong number. ## The loaded cost of automation for the same slice

**Software quotes hide the opposite costs: the build, the weeks before it works properly, and the share of the task it simply refuses. Price year one separately from year two, because setup and ramp disappear after the first year and the ongoing number is much smaller.**

The formula:

```
Year one automation cost =
    setup or build fee
  + (monthly subscription x 12)
  + (internal ramp hours x your hourly value)
  + (exceptions per week x minutes each / 60 x your hourly value x 50)
```

Continuing the illustrative example, assume a booking and reminder system with a $2,500 build, $300 per month, 20 hours of your time during setup and correction, and about 3 exceptions a week at 20 minutes each that land back on you.

| Line item | Year one | Year two |
|---|---|---|
| Build or setup | $2,500 | $0 |
| Subscription (300 x 12) | $3,600 | $3,600 |
| Internal ramp (20 x 75) | $1,500 | $0 |
| Exceptions (1 hour per week x 75 x 50) | $3,750 | $3,750 |
| **Total** | **$11,350** | **$7,350** |

The other hidden variable is coverage. Assume this system handles 70 percent of the defined slice and the remaining 30 percent still needs a person. That residual is a real line item, and quotes almost never include it. The same pattern of omitted line items shows up across vendor pricing, which is why [AI cost calculator: the five buckets a quote hides](/blog/ai-cost-calculator/) is worth reading before you accept a proposal, alongside [AI implementation cost for small business: the real floor](/blog/ai-implementation-cost-small-business/) for what the minimum realistically looks like.

## The crossover: where each option wins

**Set the two loaded totals equal and solve for hours. In the illustrative numbers, automation plus a residual human overtakes a VA at roughly 9 task hours per week in year one, and around 6 hours per week once setup and ramp are behind you.**

```
Breakeven hours per week =
    year one automation cost
    / (loaded VA hourly cost x 50 x coverage fraction)
```

With $11,350, a $35.20 loaded VA hour, and 70 percent coverage, that is about 9.3 hours per week.

| Task hours per week | VA only (loaded) | Automation plus residual human | Cheaper on cost alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | $5,250 | $12,925 | VA |
| 6 | $10,500 | $14,500 | VA |
| 10 | $17,500 | $16,600 | Automation |
| 20 | $35,000 | $21,850 | Automation |
| 40 | $70,000 | $32,350 | Automation |

Run the same five steps on your own slice:

1. Define the task in one sentence and log actual hours for a week.
2. Build the loaded VA hourly cost, including your management time.
3. Price the automation for year one and year two separately.
4. Estimate coverage honestly. If the vendor will not name a number, assume 60 percent.
5. Solve for breakeven hours and compare it to your logged number.

## When the hours are not the deciding factor

**Volume decides cost, not fit. Judgment-heavy, low-volume, or undefined work stays human regardless of the math, and automating a broken process just makes the breakage faster. Cost per hour is the wrong lens when the task changes shape every week.**

Three cases where the table above does not apply. First, work that requires a decision no rule covers, such as handling an upset customer or negotiating a reschedule with a long-standing client. Second, tasks under about 2 hours a week, where neither option pays back and you should either keep doing it yourself or batch it. Third, a process that is not written down. Software cannot absorb ambiguity the way a person quietly does, and the exception rate will be far higher than any vendor estimate.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I calculate the loaded hourly cost of a virtual assistant?

Take the annual base pay for their hours, add your own management hours valued at your hourly rate, add an amortized cost for one turnover event per year, then add platform and tool fees. Divide the total by covered hours per year. In the illustrative example above, a $22 quoted rate loads to $35.20.

### Is automation always cheaper at high volume?

On cost per unit, usually. On outcome, not always. Automation only stays cheaper if the exception rate stays low. If volume rises because the work is getting more varied rather than more frequent, exceptions grow with it and the residual human hours climb back toward what you were paying before.

### What coverage percentage should I assume?

Ask the vendor what share of cases the system completes without human involvement, and ask how they measured it. If they will not answer with a number, plan for 60 percent and treat anything above that as upside. Coverage is the single assumption most likely to swing your breakeven.

### Can I use both a VA and automation?

That is usually the right answer above the crossover. Automation takes the repeatable core, and the VA handles exceptions, judgment calls, and everything outside the defined slice. The mistake is paying for both while the VA still does the automated work manually as a backup.

### How long before automation pays for itself?

Divide the year one cost by the monthly savings against your loaded VA cost. The method, including how to treat setup as a one-time drag on the first year, is worked through in [Payback period calculator: the automation version of the math](/blog/payback-period-calculator/).
