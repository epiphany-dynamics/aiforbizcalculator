**Cost per hire is the sum of external recruiting spend, internal recruiting labor, vacancy cost while the seat sits empty, and manager time spent interviewing and onboarding, divided by the number of hires. A practical range for a small business filling one role often lands between $4,000 and $15,000 all in, depending on the role and how long it stays open.**

Most small-business owners guess this number by adding up job board fees and calling it done. That understates the real cost by half or more, because the expensive parts are invisible: the manager's interview hours, the work that did not get done while the seat was empty, and the recruiter who covered the gap with overtime.

This article gives you the full formula, a worked example with clearly labeled illustrative numbers, and a way to use the result for a capacity decision. If you want to skip the spreadsheet, the [AI automation ROI calculator](/calculator/) handles similar inputs and shows payback in one view.

## The full cost per hire formula

**The complete formula is: Cost per hire = (External costs + Internal labor costs + Vacancy cost + Manager time cost) / Number of hires. Each of the four buckets uses its own sub-formula, and vacancy and manager time should stay visible as separate line items because they behave differently and respond to different fixes.**

Here is each bucket with its sub-formula:

| Bucket | What goes in it | Sub-formula |
|---|---|---|
| External costs | Job ads, agency fees, background checks, assessment tools, referral bonuses | Sum of invoices and fees |
| Internal labor | HR or owner hours on sourcing, screening, scheduling | Hours x fully loaded hourly rate |
| Vacancy cost | Lost output or revenue while the seat is empty | Daily role value x days open |
| Manager time | Interviews, debriefs, offer negotiation, onboarding oversight | Hours x fully loaded hourly rate |

For the hourly rates, do not use base salary divided by 2,080. Use a fully loaded rate that includes payroll taxes, benefits, and overhead. If you have not calculated yours yet, the method in [fully loaded labor rate: how to calculate yours](/blog/fully-loaded-labor-rate/) takes about ten minutes and typically lands 1.25 to 1.4 times base pay.

Vacancy cost needs a judgment call. For a revenue role like sales, daily role value can be quota divided by working days. For a support role, use the cost of covering the gap: overtime paid, work delayed, or customers served slower. Keep the assumption written down so you can defend it later.

## A worked example with illustrative numbers

**A 15-person services firm hiring one account coordinator over 45 days might spend roughly $2,300 externally, $1,800 in internal labor, $4,500 in vacancy cost, and $2,600 in manager time, for an illustrative total of about $11,200 for a single hire. These are hypothetical figures to show the math, not benchmarks.**

Here is the breakdown, line by line. Assume a fully loaded rate of $45 per hour for the office manager doing screening and $65 per hour for the owner doing interviews.

External costs:

- Job board postings, two boards for 30 days: $700
- Background check and skills assessment: $250
- Referral bonus paid to an employee: $1,000
- Indeed sponsored listing top-up: $350
- External subtotal: $2,300

Internal labor:

- Writing the job description and posting: 4 hours x $45 = $180
- Screening 120 resumes: 12 hours x $45 = $540
- Phone screens, 15 at 30 minutes including scheduling: 10 hours x $45 = $450
- Coordination, references, offer paperwork: 14 hours x $45 = $630
- Internal subtotal: $1,800

Vacancy cost:

- The role supports roughly $150 of billable coordination value per day, and coverage gaps meant some work shipped late or was absorbed as overtime by teammates.
- 45 days open x $150 = $6,750 gross, but the team absorbed about a third of it through overtime already counted in payroll, so the incremental vacancy cost is estimated at $4,500.

Manager time:

- Four interview rounds with the owner: 6 candidates x 2 hours average = 12 hours x $65 = $780
- Debriefs, scorecards, and deliberation: 8 hours x $65 = $520
- Offer negotiation and onboarding plan: 6 hours x $65 = $390
- First-month check-ins beyond normal management: 14 hours x $65 = $910
- Manager subtotal: $2,600

Total: $2,300 + $1,800 + $4,500 + $2,600 = $11,200 for one hire. If the firm hires three coordinators in a year with similar cycles, annual cost per hire stays near $11,200, but the shared costs like job board subscriptions spread a little thinner.

Notice what dominated: not the job ads. Vacancy and manager time made up over 60 percent of the total. That pattern is common in illustrative builds like this one, and it changes what you should optimize.

## Why vacancy and manager time deserve their own lines

**Vacancy cost falls when you hire faster, and manager time falls when you hire with fewer interview rounds and better screening. They respond to different fixes, so merging them into one line hides the lever you actually need to pull. Keep them separate in every calculation.**

If your vacancy cost is the biggest bucket, your problem is speed: slow approvals, a posting that sat for two weeks before anyone reviewed applicants, or an interview loop stretched across a month. The fix is process compression, like same-week phone screens and pre-agreed offer ranges.

If manager time dominates, your problem is filtering. Too many marginal candidates are reaching the interview stage. Better screening questions, a paid work sample, or a structured scorecard can cut interview hours in half without lowering the bar.

This is also where turnover math connects. A bad hire does not just waste this cycle's cost; it triggers the entire cycle again. The five buckets in [cost of employee turnover calculator: the five real buckets](/blog/cost-of-employee-turnover-calculator/) show how rehiring cost stacks on top of lost productivity and training, which is why shaving vacancy days often beats shaving ad spend.

## Using the number for a capacity decision

**Cost per hire becomes a decision tool when you compare it against the cost of not hiring: overtime, delayed work, or outsourced coverage for the same capacity. If covering the gap for six months costs less than hiring and the workload is temporary, do not hire. If the workload is permanent, hire and optimize the cycle.**

Run this four-step framework before opening a req:

1. Quantify the gap. How many hours per week of work are not getting done, or are being absorbed as overtime? The [FTE calculator: turn workload into real capacity](/blog/fte-calculator/) converts a task list into the fraction of a full-time role you actually need.
2. Price the hire. Use the formula above to get cost per hire, then add the fully loaded annual salary.
3. Price the alternatives. Overtime for the existing team, a contractor, or an agency covering the same hours for six months.
4. Compare on payback. If the hire pays back inside your acceptable window and the workload is durable, hire. If the workload is uncertain, bridge with a contractor and revisit.

One honest caution: automation sometimes enters this conversation as a cheaper alternative to hiring. Sometimes it is, for narrow repetitive tasks like data entry or appointment reminders. But do not treat automation as a substitute for a hire when the role requires judgment, relationships, or exception handling. The honest comparison is automation for the automatable slice of the workload plus a smaller hire for the rest. ## Where this calculation goes wrong

**The most common errors are counting only external spend, using base salary instead of loaded rates, ignoring vacancy entirely, and double-counting coverage costs in both vacancy and overtime lines. Each error skews the number by thousands of dollars in one direction or the other.**

Three mistakes to check in your own build:

- Counting only invoices. External spend is the easiest to measure and usually the smallest bucket. A $2,300 external total inside an $11,200 true cost is exactly the distortion this produces.
- Double-counting vacancy. If you already paid overtime to cover the gap, that money is either in vacancy cost or in payroll, not both. Pick one home for it and note the choice.
- Averaging across unlike roles. A blended cost per hire across a sales rep and an admin hides more than it reveals. Calculate by role family: revenue roles, operations roles, and specialist roles each get their own number.

Also track your numbers over time so the illustrative estimates above get replaced by your own data. After two or three hiring cycles, your vacancy-days average and manager-hour totals will be far more accurate than any generic figure, including the ones in this article. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a good cost per hire for a small business?

There is no universal good number because role, seniority, and time-to-fill drive the total. Illustrative all-in builds for a single small-business hire often land between $4,000 and $15,000. Your own tracked number, compared against itself over time, matters more than any outside benchmark.

### Should vacancy cost really count as a hiring cost?

Yes, if you keep it honest. The empty seat has a real price in lost output, overtime, or delayed work. Estimate it conservatively, write down your assumption, and avoid counting costs that already appear elsewhere, like overtime you also put in payroll.

### How do I calculate manager time without tracking every minute?

Estimate per stage: hours per phone screen, per interview round, per debrief, multiplied by the number of candidates. Have the manager sanity-check the totals against their calendar for one recent hire, then apply that template going forward.

### Does cost per hire include salary?

No. Cost per hire covers the expense of finding and landing the person, not their ongoing compensation. Salary belongs in your capacity decision comparison, where you weigh total first-year cost of the hire against the alternatives.

### How can I lower cost per hire without lowering quality?

Compress the cycle to cut vacancy days, tighten screening to cut interview hours, and build a referral pipeline to cut ad spend. Speed and filtering improvements usually save more than negotiating cheaper job board rates.

### When should I not hire, even if the math supports it?

When the workload is temporary, uncertain, or likely to shrink within the payback window. Also pause if you cannot articulate what the person will do in week one; an undefined role inflates every cost bucket and raises the odds of a costly rehire.
