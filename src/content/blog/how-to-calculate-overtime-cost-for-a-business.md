**Overtime cost equals overtime hours multiplied by 1.5 times the employee's regular hourly rate, plus employer payroll taxes on that pay. For a true number, start from the fully loaded labor rate, track overtime as a percentage of total payroll, and compare the annual premium against hiring or automation before approving more hours.**

## The core overtime cost formula

**The basic formula is overtime hours times the regular hourly rate times 1.5, which is the federal Fair Labor Standards Act requirement for non-exempt employees who work more than 40 hours in a workweek. Multiply gross overtime pay by 1.0765 to add the employer share of Social Security and Medicare taxes.**

### The simple version

Overtime pay = overtime hours x regular hourly rate x 1.5

Illustrative example: an employee earning $20 per hour works 46 hours in one workweek. That is 40 straight-time hours and 6 overtime hours.

- Overtime pay: 6 x $20 x 1.5 = $180
- Straight-time pay: 40 x $20 = $800
- Gross pay for the week: $980

The 1.5 multiplier is the federal floor for non-exempt employees. Some states add daily overtime rules, such as time-and-a-half after 8 hours in a day. Where state law is more generous than federal law, the state rule wins, so confirm your state's requirements before building policy around the 40-hour number alone.

### The version that includes employer taxes

Wages are not the whole cost. The employer share of FICA, 6.2 percent for Social Security and 1.45 percent for Medicare, applies to overtime pay the same as straight-time pay:

True overtime cost = overtime hours x regular rate x 1.5 x 1.0765

For the six hours above: $180 x 1.0765 = $193.77. State unemployment tax and workers compensation premiums often scale with wages too, which is why the loaded rate matters, covered below.

## A worked example with real numbers

**A five-person team where each person works six overtime hours a week at $22 per hour costs about $1,066 per week once employer payroll taxes are included, or roughly $55,400 per year. That annual figure is the number to compare against the cost of a new hire or an automation subscription.**

This illustrative example uses a small fulfillment operation. Every number is a placeholder; swap in your own.

1. Count the overtime hours. Five employees each work 6 overtime hours: 5 x 6 = 30 overtime hours per week.
2. Apply the multiplier. 30 x $22 x 1.5 = $990 in weekly overtime wages.
3. Add employer payroll taxes. $990 x 7.65 percent = $75.74, so the weekly overtime cost is about $1,066.
4. Annualize. $1,066 x 52 = about $55,400 per year in overtime cost.
5. Isolate the premium. Those hours would cost straight-time pay no matter who works them, so the true cost of choosing overtime is the extra half: 30 x $22 x 0.5 = $330 per week, or about $18,500 per year after taxes.
6. Compute the overtime share. If total weekly payroll including overtime is $12,000, overtime is $990 / $12,000 = 8.3 percent of payroll.

### Reading the result

Thirty overtime hours a week is three-quarters of a full-time position. This business is paying new-hire money, $55,400 a year, without gaining a new hire's 40 hours of capacity or coverage. The $18,500 premium is the cleanest comparison number, because the other $36,900 is straight-time pay that any worker covering those hours would receive.

## Use the fully loaded rate, not the wage

**The rate you multiply by 1.5 should reflect what an hour truly costs, not just the wage. Employer payroll taxes, workers compensation premiums, and any pay-linked benefits ride on top of overtime wages, so calculating from the base wage alone understates every overtime hour you approve.**

Illustrative loaded-rate build for the same $22 employee:

| Cost component | Straight-time hour | Overtime hour |
|---|---|---|
| Base pay | $22.00 | $33.00 |
| Employer FICA (7.65%) | $1.68 | $2.52 |
| Workers compensation (illustrative 3% of wages) | $0.66 | $0.99 |
| Pay-linked benefits (illustrative) | $0.90 | $0.90 |
| Loaded cost per hour | $25.24 | $37.41 |

Two cautions. First, workers compensation and unemployment rates vary widely by state and industry, so treat these percentages as placeholders. Second, only include costs that actually rise with hours or pay. A fixed monthly health premium does not get more expensive when someone works overtime, so it belongs in your average loaded rate but not in the marginal overtime calculation. ## Overtime versus hiring versus automation

**Compare the annual overtime premium, the extra half time plus taxes on every overtime hour, against the fully loaded cost of a new hire and the full cost of automating the work. The cheapest option changes with overtime volume, so run all three numbers before deciding.**

Here is the illustrative comparison for the fulfillment team with 30 weekly overtime hours:

| Option | Illustrative annual cost | Best when | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Keep paying overtime | $55,400 total; $18,500 premium | Extra hours are seasonal or unpredictable | Burnout, errors, turnover |
| Hire at $22 per hour | About $49,300 in wages and FICA, before benefits | The work is steady at 30 or more hours weekly | Fixed cost in slow months |
| Automate the workflow | Price your actual stack; small-team software budgets are often a few hundred dollars a month | The work is repetitive and rules-based | Setup time and edge cases |

The hiring line gives you 40 hours of capacity for roughly the price of 30 overtime hours, but only if the work is there every week. ## When overtime is the wrong number to cut

**Overtime is often cheaper than hiring when the extra hours are seasonal, sporadic, or modest, because a new employee adds fixed costs like benefits, recruiting, training, and management time even in weeks when there is no work to give them. Do not cut overtime that is buying you real flexibility.**

The limitation of the premium math above is that it assumes the work is steady. If your overtime spikes for eight weeks around the holidays and disappears otherwise, the annual premium overstates the problem, because a hire would sit idle the other 44 weeks while still collecting pay and benefits. Overtime is expensive per hour but flexible per week, and flexibility has value the hourly comparison ignores.

Two more cautions. First, never chase overtime savings by reclassifying non-exempt employees as exempt or as contractors. Misclassification is a wage-and-hour violation with back-pay exposure, not a cost strategy. Second, chronic overtime carries costs the formula misses: fatigue-driven errors, slower work late in long shifts, and turnover that forces recruiting and training spend. If the same people are on overtime every week of the year, the problem is structural, and the hiring or automation columns deserve a serious look.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do you calculate overtime pay for an hourly employee?

Multiply overtime hours by 1.5 times the regular hourly rate, then add the result to straight-time pay. Under federal law, overtime hours are those beyond 40 in a workweek for non-exempt employees; some states also count hours beyond 8 in a day. Add the 7.65 percent employer FICA share to get the cost to the business rather than just the pay to the worker.

### What is the overtime premium, and why does it matter?

The premium is the extra half time above the straight-time rate: overtime hours times the regular rate times 0.5. Those hours would cost straight-time pay no matter who works them, so the premium is the true incremental cost of choosing overtime over hiring or automating. It is the cleanest figure for any comparison.

### Is there a standard overtime percentage a business should target?

There is no durable universal benchmark, because overtime norms depend on industry, seasonality, and labor market. Calculate your overtime share as overtime pay divided by total payroll, then watch the trend over several months. A share that keeps climbing usually signals structural understaffing or a process problem, and the fix lives in the hiring, staffing, or automation math rather than in chasing a generic target.

### Do salaried employees get overtime?

Many do. A salary alone does not make an employee exempt; federal rules require meeting both a salary threshold and specific duties tests, and some states set higher bars. If a salaried employee is non-exempt, you owe overtime on hours over 40 in a workweek, with the regular rate derived from the salary. Confirm classifications with your payroll provider or an employment attorney.

### How do you calculate overtime for an employee with two pay rates?

Use a blended regular rate: total straight-time earnings divided by total hours worked, then add half that rate for each overtime hour. Illustrative example: 30 hours at $20 plus 16 hours at $24 is $984 over 46 hours, a $21.39 blended rate. The overtime premium is 6 x $21.39 x 0.5 = $64.17, for total gross pay of $1,048.17.

### Do bonuses count in the overtime rate?

Nondiscretionary bonuses, such as production or attendance bonuses promised in advance, must be included in the regular rate under federal rules, which raises the overtime rate slightly. Truly discretionary bonuses generally are excluded. If you pay any form of incentive, confirm the treatment with your payroll provider before running overtime calculations.
