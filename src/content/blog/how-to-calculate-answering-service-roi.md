**Answering service ROI = (recovered answered calls x average value per call) minus monthly service cost, divided by monthly service cost. If a $400/month service recovers 30 calls worth $80 each, that is $2,400 in recovered value against $400 in cost, an ROI of 500%.**

## The core formula

**Answering service ROI compares what you get back (calls answered that would otherwise go to voicemail or a competitor) against what you pay. The formula is ROI = (recovered call value minus service cost) / service cost, expressed as a percentage or ratio.**

Written out:

```
Recovered call value = recovered calls x average value per call
Net gain = recovered call value - monthly service cost
ROI (%) = (net gain / monthly service cost) x 100
```

Two numbers do all the work here: how many calls the service actually recovers, and what each of those calls is worth to you. Everything else in this article is about pinning down those two inputs honestly instead of guessing.

## Step 1: count recovered answered calls

**Recovered calls are the calls that would have gone unanswered without the service, not your total call volume. Track baseline missed calls before the service starts, then compare to missed calls after, using your phone system's call log or the service's monthly report.**

Most business owners overstate this number by counting every call the service handles, including ones a receptionist or owner would have caught anyway. The honest way to isolate the recovery number:

1. Pull 30 days of call logs before signing up. Count total calls, answered calls, and missed calls (voicemail, abandoned, after-hours).
2. Pull the same 30-day window after the service is live.
3. Subtract: recovered calls = (missed calls before) minus (missed calls after).
4. If call volume changed for unrelated reasons (seasonality, a marketing push), normalize by dividing by total calls to get a missed-call rate, then compare rates instead of raw counts.

**Illustrative example:** A small HVAC company logs 500 calls a month before adding an answering service, with 90 going to voicemail. After the service starts, only 15 calls go unanswered. Recovered calls = 90 minus 15 = 75 per month. That is the number that goes into the formula, not the 200+ calls the service technically touched.

For a deeper walkthrough of what an unanswered call actually costs before you even get to recovery math, see [how to calculate the cost of a missed call](/blog/how-to-calculate-the-cost-of-a-missed-call/).

## Step 2: assign a value per recovered call

**Average value per call is your close rate times your average job or ticket value, not your total revenue divided by call count. Use your own conversion data from booked jobs, not an industry estimate, since close rates vary widely by trade and by whether the caller is new or returning.**

The formula:

```
Value per call = close rate (as a decimal) x average job value
```

**Illustrative example, continued:** The same HVAC company closes 35% of answered calls into booked jobs, and average job value is $310. Value per recovered call = 0.35 x $310 = $108.50.

Applying that to the 75 recovered calls: recovered call value = 75 x $108.50 = $8,137.50 per month.

Two adjustments matter for accuracy:

- **New vs. existing customers.** A missed call from an existing customer often gets recovered anyway (they call back or you follow up). Weight new-lead calls higher since those are the ones genuinely lost without the service.
- **Emergency vs. routine calls.** After-hours and urgent calls often carry higher close rates because the caller has fewer alternatives at that moment. If your service mainly covers after-hours, use your after-hours close rate, not your daytime average.

## Step 3: subtract the real service cost

**Service cost is the full monthly bill, not just the base plan. Add per-minute or per-call overage charges, setup fees amortized over the contract term, and any integration or CRM sync fees the vendor bills separately.**

Answering service pricing is rarely one flat number. A typical structure:

| Cost component | Example (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| Base plan (included minutes) | $300/month |
| Overage minutes | $0.85/min beyond plan |
| Setup/onboarding fee | $250 one-time |
| CRM integration fee | $50/month |
| **Total month 1** | **$600 + overage** |
| **Total steady-state (month 2+)** | **$350 + overage** |

If the HVAC company above averages 40 overage minutes at $0.85, that adds $34/month, bringing steady-state cost to $384.

Net gain = $8,137.50 (recovered call value) minus $384 (cost) = $7,753.50.

ROI = ($7,753.50 / $384) x 100 = roughly 2,019%.

That number looks extreme, and it can be, when close rates and job values are high relative to a modest monthly fee. It is also exactly why isolating true recovered calls in Step 1 matters so much: inflate that number and the ROI figure becomes meaningless. **A side-by-side table makes it easy to see how sensitive ROI is to close rate and job value, which is why generic industry ROI claims are unreliable. Run your own numbers using the framework above before trusting any published benchmark.**

| Business type (illustrative) | Recovered calls/mo | Close rate | Avg job value | Recovered value | Service cost | ROI |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| HVAC contractor | 75 | 35% | $310 | $8,137.50 | $384 | 2,019% |

## When this math does not apply

**This formula assumes missed calls actually represent lost revenue, which is not true for every business. If most of your missed calls are spam, wrong numbers, or repeat calls from the same customer trying to reach you, recovered-call ROI will overstate the benefit.**

Before running the calculation, filter out:

- Robocalls and spam, which many phone systems already flag separately
- Duplicate calls from the same number within a short window
- Internal or vendor calls unrelated to customer acquisition

If after filtering your missed-call rate was already under 5%, an answering service may cost more than the calls it recovers are worth, and the better fix might be a callback system or fixing hold times instead. ## Frequently asked questions

### What counts as a "recovered" call versus a call the service just handled?

A recovered call is one that would have gone to voicemail or been abandoned without the service. If your front desk or owner would have answered it anyway, it is not recovered, it is just handled by a different person. Compare missed-call counts before and after the service to isolate the true recovery number.

### How long should I track data before trusting the ROI number?

Use at least 60 days of post-service data. The first 30 days often include setup adjustments, script tuning, and caller confusion that temporarily depress or inflate the numbers. A second full month gives a steadier baseline for close rate and call volume.

### Does this formula work for AI-based answering services too?

Yes, the formula is the same regardless of whether a human or an AI system answers the call. The only difference is usually cost structure (AI services often price per-minute or per-resolution rather than per-seat) and sometimes a different close rate, since some callers respond differently to an automated system. Run the numbers separately rather than assuming parity. For a fuller comparison of what an AI receptionist changes about the math, see is an AI receptionist cheaper than hiring? The real math.

### What if my close rate varies a lot by season?

Calculate ROI separately for peak and off-peak months if your business has strong seasonality, then use a weighted annual average rather than one month's snapshot. A single busy-season month will overstate annual ROI, and a single slow month will understate it.

### Should I include the cost of the calls I would have missed anyway if I hadn't hired anyone?

No. Compare the answering service against your actual current state (whatever coverage you have now, including none), not against a hypothetical in-house hire.
