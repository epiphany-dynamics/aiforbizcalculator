**At small-business scale, most AI implementations land between a few hundred and a few thousand dollars in year one, because you are buying a subscription and configuring it. The five and six figure ranges you see quoted are custom software builds. Those are a different product, priced for a different buyer.**

Search "AI implementation cost" and you get ranges that start around five thousand dollars, run to a quarter million, and sometimes carry a five-year total in the hundreds of thousands. If you have five employees and one phone line, none of that describes your situation. The problem is not that the numbers are invented. The problem is that they are averages across buyers who have nothing in common with you.

## Why the published ranges do not apply to you

**Those ranges blend two unrelated purchases: buying seats in software that already exists, and commissioning software that does not. Averaging them produces a number that describes neither. A quote near two hundred fifty thousand dollars is a development contract with discovery, data engineering, and a maintenance retainer attached, not the price of turning on a feature.**

The tell is in what the quote includes. Enterprise implementation pricing assumes requirements gathering across departments, integration with systems that have no public API, security review, staged rollout, training for dozens of users, and a support agreement. Every one of those line items exists because the buyer has scale and complexity. A three-person dental office has neither. If you want the complete driver-by-driver breakdown behind these numbers, the [AI Automation Pricing Guide: What Drives Cost for Small Businesses](https://epiphanydynamics.ai/blog/ai-automation-cost-pricing-guide/) walks through each one.

When you strip out the enterprise assumptions, what is left is usually one of three things: a subscription, a setup fee, and your own hours. That is the entire cost structure for most small-business AI work. Anything above it needs a specific reason, and the reason should be something you can name out loud.

## The three buying modes and what each actually costs

**There are only three ways to get AI into a small business: adopt a finished tool, configure a platform, or commission a build. The cost gap between the first and the third is roughly two orders of magnitude, and most published ranges quote the third while readers assume the first.**

| Buying mode | What you get | First-year cost shape (illustrative) | Fits when | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Adopt | A finished product you sign up for and switch on | Monthly subscription only, often double or low triple digits per month | Your need is common: call answering, reminders, scheduling, transcription | The tool does 80 percent of what you want and you live with the gap |
| Configure | A platform plus someone wiring it to your systems | Subscription plus a one-time setup fee, often several hundred to a few thousand | You need two or three existing systems to talk to each other | Setup cost is quoted low and change requests are billed later |
| Build | Custom software written for your process | Five figures and up, plus ongoing maintenance | Your process is genuinely unusual and it is a real profit driver | You now own software, including its upkeep |

Most owners who think they need a build actually need a configure. The instinct comes from describing the workflow in detail and hearing an agency say "we can build that," when the honest answer was that three off-the-shelf tools and a connector would have covered it. ## The real floor at five employees

**The floor is not zero, but it is far lower than published ranges imply. For a business with a handful of staff and one repetitive task, a realistic first-year outlay is a subscription plus a setup fee plus somewhere between five and twenty hours of your own time. That is the honest bottom of the market.**

What the floor actually includes:

- **Subscription.** One tool, one seat or one line, billed monthly. Cancelable.
- **Setup.** Either your own hours or a one-time fee to someone who has done it before.
- **Connection cost.** If the tool needs to write into your scheduler or CRM, either a native integration exists or you pay for a connector. Automation platform pricing is its own small budget line, and [Make.com Cost for Small Business: Plans, Pricing, and What You Actually Need](https://epiphanydynamics.ai/blog/make-com-cost-small-business/) covers how those plans scale with volume.
- **Usage.** Per-minute, per-message, or per-run charges that move with your volume.
- **Your attention.** The line item nobody quotes. Testing, correcting, and rewriting instructions in the first month.

What people forget and then get surprised by: the second month of tuning, the annual price increase, and the cost of the person who has to answer questions when the tool gets something wrong. None of those are large individually. Together they can double a naive estimate.

## A formula for your first-year number

**Stop asking what AI costs and start pricing your specific project. One formula covers every buying mode, and it takes about ten minutes to fill in. If a vendor will not give you the inputs, that refusal is the most useful information you will get from the conversation.**

First-year cost = (monthly subscription x 12) + one-time setup + (your hours x loaded hourly rate) + expected usage charges + connector or platform fees

Illustrative example, a five-person clinic adding after-hours call answering. These figures are hypothetical and used only to show the arithmetic:

- Subscription: 150 per month, so 1,800 for the year
- Setup fee: 750 one time
- Internal time: 12 hours at a 45 dollar loaded rate, so 540
- Usage overage: 40 per month on busy months, call it 300
- Connector: 20 per month, so 240

First-year total: 3,630. Nowhere near the published floor of 5,000, and nowhere near a quarter million. Now run it against what the tool is supposed to save. If the clinic books three additional appointments a month at 180 each, that is 6,480 in year-one revenue against 3,630 in cost.

## When the big number is the right number

**Sometimes the five-figure quote is correct and the cheap option is a waste. If your process is the thing customers pay you for, if it touches regulated data, or if failure creates liability rather than inconvenience, buying the cheapest tool and hoping is the more expensive decision.**

Signals that you genuinely belong in build territory: your workflow has no off-the-shelf equivalent because it is your competitive advantage, you have compliance obligations that dictate where data lives, or the volume is high enough that per-unit pricing on a subscription exceeds a build's amortized cost. Those are real cases. They are just not most cases.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the cheapest realistic AI implementation for a small business?

A single subscription tool solving one narrow, repetitive task, set up by you in an afternoon. That means a monthly fee and your own hours, with no setup fee and no consultant. It works when your need is common enough that a finished product already exists for it.

### Why do agencies quote five figures when tools cost double digits per month?

Because they are quoting labor, not software. The subscription is a pass-through; the fee covers discovery, configuration, integration, testing, and support. That work is real. Whether you need all of it depends on how unusual your process is and how much of the setup you are willing to do yourself.

### Should I count my own time as a cost?

Yes, at a loaded rate that includes payroll taxes and benefits. Owner time is the most commonly omitted input and often the largest one in a self-serve implementation. Leaving it out makes cheap options look free and skews the comparison against paying someone to do it properly.

### How long should payback take before I walk away?

For a subscription tool, if the monthly benefit does not exceed the monthly cost within about three months, the fit is wrong. For a project with a meaningful setup fee, twelve months is a reasonable ceiling. Beyond that, too many assumptions have to hold for the math to survive.

### Do the five-year total cost figures mean anything at my size?

Not much. Five-year totals assume a fixed platform, a fixed headcount, and a stable vendor. At small-business scale, you should be able to cancel within a month or two. Price the first year, keep the exit cheap, and reassess annually.
