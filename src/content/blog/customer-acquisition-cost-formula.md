**Customer acquisition cost (CAC) equals total sales and marketing spend divided by new customers acquired in the same period. The formula is simple, but blended CAC (all spend, all customers) and paid-channel CAC (only paid ads, only the customers they drove) tell very different stories, and mixing them up hides your true cost per customer.**

## The CAC formula, line by line

**CAC = total acquisition spend divided by number of new customers acquired in that period. Acquisition spend includes ad spend, sales salaries and commissions, marketing software fees, and agency or content costs. New customers means people who started paying in that window, not leads, trials, or email signups that never converted.**

Getting the formula right depends on getting both sides of the fraction right.

### What counts as spend

Include every dollar tied to getting a customer to say yes: paid ad budgets, the fully loaded cost of sales and marketing staff (salary, payroll tax, benefits), commissions paid on closed deals, CRM and marketing automation subscriptions, and any freelance or agency fees for the same period. Leave out product development costs and customer success spend that happens after the sale; those belong in retention or expansion metrics, not acquisition.

### What counts as a new customer

Count someone the moment they become a paying customer, not when they fill out a form. If your sales cycle is 45 days, match spend from the period that generated the lead to the customer count from the period they actually closed, or you will understate CAC in slow months and overstate it in fast ones.

## Blended CAC vs paid-channel CAC

**Blended CAC divides all sales and marketing spend by all new customers, including referrals and organic search. Paid-channel CAC isolates one channel, dividing only that channel's spend by the customers it directly drove. Blended CAC tells you overall efficiency; paid-channel CAC tells you whether a specific campaign deserves more budget.**

Both numbers matter, and they answer different questions. Here is how they compare in practice.

| Metric | Formula | What it includes | Best used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blended CAC | Total spend / total new customers | Paid ads, organic, referral, direct | Overall efficiency, board reporting |
| Paid-channel CAC | Channel spend / channel-attributed customers | Only one paid source (e.g., Google Ads) | Deciding whether to scale or cut a channel |
| Fully loaded CAC | (Ad spend + salaries + tools) / new customers | Everything above plus overhead | True profitability per customer |

If you only track blended CAC, a strong referral program can mask a paid channel that is quietly losing money. If you only track paid-channel CAC, you will miss the salary and software costs that make the real number higher than the dashboard suggests.

## A worked example: plugging in real spend

**Using illustrative numbers, a service business spends $4,000 on paid ads, $3,000 on a part-time marketing contractor, and $500 on software in one month, then closes 15 new customers. Blended CAC is $7,500 divided by 15, or $500 per customer. If $4,000 of that spend and 8 of those customers came from paid ads alone, paid-channel CAC is $500, coincidentally the same in this case.**

Change one variable and the picture shifts. If those same 8 paid customers actually cost $4,000 but the contractor and software supported only the other 7 customers (from referrals and content), your paid-channel CAC is $4,000 / 8 = $500, while the CAC for the non-paid channels is $3,500 / 7 = $500 as well. Now imagine the contractor drove only 2 of those customers: their effective CAC is $1,750, far higher than the paid channel, which would tell you to reallocate that budget toward ads instead. This is illustrative math, but it shows why splitting spend by channel before dividing matters more than the total spend figure alone.

## Common mistakes that distort your CAC number

**The most common CAC errors are counting leads instead of paying customers, forgetting fully loaded salary costs, mismatching the spend period to the conversion period, and averaging across channels that behave nothing alike. Any one of these can swing your reported CAC by 30 percent or more without any real change in performance.**

Run through this checklist before you trust a CAC figure:

1. Are you counting customers who paid, not just leads or trials?
2. Does the spend period match when those customers actually converted?
3. Did you include salaries, commissions, and software, not just ad spend?
4. Are you separating paid-channel CAC from blended CAC, or reporting only one?
5. Have you excluded churned customers who came back (they are not new acquisitions)?

## When CAC alone won't tell you the whole story

**CAC by itself does not tell you if a customer is profitable. A $500 CAC is a bargain if that customer's lifetime value is $5,000, and a disaster if lifetime value is $400. Never evaluate CAC without also knowing average order value, retention length, and gross margin per customer.**

This is the exception worth flagging clearly: businesses with long sales cycles or high-touch service models (consulting, custom fabrication, B2B contracts) should pair CAC with a payback period calculation, since a low CAC paired with a 24-month payback can be worse than a higher CAC that pays back in 3 months. Treat CAC as one input, not the whole verdict.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a good CAC for a small business?

There is no universal benchmark; a good CAC depends entirely on your average order value, margin, and how long a customer typically stays. A useful rule of thumb is comparing CAC to lifetime value: many businesses aim for lifetime value at least three times CAC, but confirm this against your own numbers rather than an industry average.

### How is CAC different from cost per lead?

Cost per lead measures spend divided by leads generated, regardless of whether they ever buy. CAC measures spend divided by customers who actually converted to paying status. A campaign can have a low cost per lead and a high CAC if few of those leads ever close.

### Should I include existing customer upsells in CAC?

No. CAC should only include spend and customers tied to acquiring someone new. Upsell and expansion revenue from existing customers belongs in a separate expansion revenue or net revenue retention calculation, not the acquisition cost formula.

### How often should I recalculate CAC?

Monthly is typical for businesses with short sales cycles and frequent ad spend changes. If your sales cycle runs longer than 60 days, a quarterly recalculation that matches spend to the correct conversion cohort will be more accurate than a monthly snapshot.

### Can CAC go negative or be misleading if referrals are strong?

CAC cannot go negative, but a strong referral program can make blended CAC look artificially low while hiding a paid channel that is losing money. Always check paid-channel CAC separately before deciding to scale ad spend.
