**Customer acquisition cost (CAC) is the total sales and marketing spend needed to win one new paying customer, calculated as acquisition spend divided by new customers in the same period. Keep retention spend out of it, price each lead separately, and compare CAC to gross profit per customer to decide whether growth pays.**

Most owners know roughly what they spend on marketing. Fewer know what one new customer costs them. That gap is where money leaks, because a channel that feels busy can still lose money on every sale.

This guide defines CAC, shows which expenses belong in it, and walks through pricing each lead. All dollar figures are illustrative, built around a made-up home cleaning company, so swap in your own.

## What customer acquisition cost means

**CAC is what you spend, in total, to turn a stranger into a first-time paying customer. It includes ads, sales time, tools, and commissions tied to winning the sale. It excludes what you spend keeping existing customers. One number, one period, one count of new customers.**

The word "total" matters. Ad spend alone is not CAC. If you pay $1,800 for ads but also spend 20 hours of your own time quoting jobs, that time is part of the cost of winning the customer, even if no invoice shows it.

### What usually counts

- Paid ads, sponsored listings, and lead-service fees
- Sales or estimator time, valued at what that person costs you per hour
- Commissions and referral bonuses paid for new business
- Lead-tracking software, call tracking, and quoting tools
- Travel or materials for free estimates

### What usually does not count

- Delivering the service itself (that is cost of goods, not acquisition)
- Rent, general admin, and bookkeeping unrelated to sales
- Anything aimed only at existing customers, covered next

## Acquisition spend versus retention spend

**Acquisition spend exists to win a customer who has not bought yet. Retention spend exists to keep or grow customers you already have. Mixing them makes CAC look either too low or too high, so assign every expense line to one bucket before you divide anything.**

A simple test: would you still spend this dollar if you stopped seeking new customers tomorrow? If yes, it is probably retention or overhead. If no, it is acquisition.

| Expense (illustrative monthly) | Amount | Bucket | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Local search ads | $1,800 | Acquisition | Targets people who have not bought |
| Referral bonuses for new clients | $300 | Acquisition | Paid only when a new customer signs |
| Estimator time, 20 hrs at $25 | $500 | Acquisition | Time spent quoting new prospects |
| Lead-tracking tool | $150 | Acquisition | Tracks new inquiries |
| Mileage to free estimates | $250 | Acquisition | Exists only to win the job |
| Review-request texts to past clients | $60 | Retention | Aimed at existing customers |
| Holiday cards to current clients | $90 | Retention | Relationship upkeep |
| Loyalty discount on repeat cleanings | $400 | Retention | Rewards customers already won |

Acquisition lines total $3,000. Retention lines total $550. If you lumped them together and divided by 10 new customers, you would report $355 instead of $300, and you would blame your ads for what is really a loyalty program.

Some spend does both jobs. Review requests, for example, also help future prospects trust you. Pick a consistent rule (most owners assign it by primary purpose) and keep it the same every month so the trend stays meaningful.

## The CAC formula and a worked example

**CAC equals total acquisition spend in a period divided by new customers won in that same period. Example: $3,000 of acquisition spend and 10 new customers gives a CAC of $300. Then compare first-year gross profit per customer to CAC to see whether that acquisition paid back.**

The formula:

**CAC = total acquisition spend ÷ new customers acquired**

Both numbers must cover the same window. If a lead paid for in March closes in May, a single month can mislead, so many owners use a rolling three-month window.

### A numbered way to run it

1. List every expense from the last full period.
2. Tag each line as acquisition, retention, or unrelated overhead.
3. Add up the acquisition lines.
4. Count customers who bought for the first time in that period. Repeat buyers do not count.
5. Divide spend by that count.
6. Compare the result to gross profit per customer.

### Checking whether $300 is good

Suppose each new customer spends $900 in their first year at a 55 percent gross margin. Gross profit is $900 × 0.55 = $495.

- Gross profit to CAC ratio: $495 ÷ $300 = 1.65
- Monthly gross profit per customer: $495 ÷ 12 = $41.25
- Payback: $300 ÷ $41.25 ≈ 7.3 months

That is workable if customers stay past seven months. If half leave after three, it is not. Gross profit is the right yardstick here, not revenue, because revenue ignores what it costs to deliver the work.

## Price each new lead before it becomes a customer

**Divide acquisition spend by leads to get cost per lead, then divide that by your close rate to reach CAC. In the illustrative example, $3,000 across 40 leads is $75 per lead, and a 25 percent close rate returns the same $300 CAC.**

Pricing the lead matters because leads are what you can observe daily. You will not know this week's CAC, but you can see this week's cost per lead and adjust.

**Cost per lead = acquisition spend ÷ leads**
**CAC = cost per lead ÷ close rate**

Check: $75 ÷ 0.25 = $300. Ten customers from 40 leads.

### Split it by channel

An average hides which source earns its keep. Here are the same 40 leads by channel. Direct spend is what each channel costs on its own. The remaining $900 (estimator time, tool, mileage) is shared, so I split it evenly at $90 per customer.

| Channel | Direct spend | Leads | Customers | Direct cost per lead | CAC with shared $90 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Local search ads | $1,800 | 20 | 4 | $90.00 | $540 |
| Referrals | $300 | 8 | 4 | $37.50 | $165 |
| Neighborhood social posts | $0 | 12 | 2 | $0 | $90 |
| **Total** | **$2,100** | **40** | **10** | | **$300 blended** |

The blended $300 looks fine. The channel view shows ads at $540 against $495 first-year gross profit, so that channel loses money in year one, while referrals win easily. That is a decision you cannot make from the average.

A $90 lead that goes to voicemail is money burned before the sale starts. If you pay per inquiry, response speed feeds directly into close rate, and close rate feeds directly into CAC. ## When CAC misleads you

**CAC is unreliable on tiny samples, long sales cycles, and businesses that grow mostly by word of mouth. It also says nothing about lifetime value. Treat it as a direction, not a verdict, and do not cut a channel on one thin month of data.**

Watch for these limits:

- **Small counts.** Two customers in a month means one extra sale swings CAC by a large percentage. Use a rolling window.
- **Long sales cycles.** If deals take 90 days, this quarter's spend produced last quarter's customers, or next quarter's. Offset the periods.
- **Free channels that are not free.** Your own posting time is real cost. Leaving it out flatters organic channels.
- **Assisted wins.** A customer may see an ad, then get a referral, then call. Last-touch attribution gives all credit to one source. Use channel CAC to spot extremes, not to settle small differences.
- **Lifetime value.** A 7-month payback on a client who stays for years is excellent. The same payback on a one-time job is not. CAC has no meaning without knowing how long customers stay and how much they buy.

When not to lean on it: if you are under about 15 to 20 new customers per period, use CAC as a rough guide and lean on cost per lead and close rate, which have more data behind them.

## Using CAC in cost and ROI decisions

**Acquisition costs are one bucket in any spending decision. If you are evaluating software or automation to lower CAC, count its full cost, then test the claimed gains against your own close rate and lead volume, not a vendor's promised figures.**

If a tool claims to lift close rate, the CAC formula tells you what that is worth. Raising close rate from 25 to 30 percent at $75 per lead drops CAC from $300 to $250, a $50 saving per customer, or $500 a month at 10 customers. Compare that to the tool's real monthly cost.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the simple meaning of customer acquisition cost?

It is the average amount you spend to win one new paying customer. Add up the sales and marketing costs tied to winning new business in a period, then divide by the number of first-time customers in that period.

### Does CAC include salaries?

Include the portion of salary or owner time spent on winning new customers, such as quoting, follow-up calls, and sales meetings. Do not include time spent delivering the service or running general operations.

### What is the difference between CAC and cost per lead?

Cost per lead is spend divided by inquiries. CAC is spend divided by customers who actually bought. CAC equals cost per lead divided by close rate, so a low cost per lead can still produce a high CAC if few leads close.

### Is a lower CAC always better?

No. A low CAC from customers who buy once and leave can be worse than a higher CAC from customers who stay for years. Compare CAC against gross profit per customer and payback time, not on its own.

### How often should I calculate CAC?

Monthly is a good habit, viewed as a rolling three-month average so a single unusual month does not distort it. Track cost per lead weekly if you run paid ads.
