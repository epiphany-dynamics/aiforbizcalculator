**A business margin calculator only helps if you know which margin it is returning. Gross margin, operating margin, and contribution margin answer different questions, and automation decisions usually need contribution margin rather than gross. This page gives the three formulas, a worked example, and the decision rules for each number.**

## The three margins a calculator should separate

**Gross margin shows what is left after direct costs. Operating margin shows what is left after overhead. Contribution margin shows what one additional sale adds toward fixed costs and profit. A calculator that returns a single blended margin hides these differences, and those differences change the answer to most spending questions.**

| Margin | Formula | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Gross margin | (Revenue - Cost of goods sold) / Revenue | Is the product or service priced above its direct cost? |
| Operating margin | (Revenue - COGS - Operating expenses) / Revenue | Does the whole business run profitably? |
| Contribution margin | (Revenue - Variable costs) / Revenue, or Price - Variable cost per unit | Does one more sale cover its own variable cost? |

### Gross margin

Cost of goods sold means the direct materials and direct labor required to deliver what you sold. A 55 percent gross margin means every revenue dollar leaves 55 cents after producing the thing. Gross margin is a pricing health check, and the [Gross Profit Calculator: Formula, Examples, and Automation Use](/blog/gross-profit-calculator/) walks through it in more depth.

### Operating margin

Operating expenses are the costs that exist whether or not you sell anything this week: rent, salaried admin staff, software subscriptions, insurance, and marketing. Subtract them from gross profit and divide by revenue. This is the number that tells you whether the business model works, not just the product.

### Contribution margin

Variable costs move with each sale: job materials, hourly labor on that job, payment processing fees, and per-job fuel or delivery. Contribution margin strips out only those costs, which makes it the right number for incremental questions like whether one more booking is worth taking at a discount, or what a recovered sale is actually worth.

## A worked example from one set of books

**Illustrative example: a residential cleaning company with $40,000 in monthly revenue produces three different margins from the same books. Its gross margin is 55 percent, its operating margin is 20 percent, and its contribution margin is 50 percent. Each number is correct, and each answers a different question.**

The numbers behind those results:

- Revenue: $40,000
- Direct costs (cleaner wages and supplies): $18,000
- Gross profit: $40,000 - $18,000 = $22,000, so gross margin = $22,000 / $40,000 = 55 percent
- Operating expenses (office rent $3,000, salaried admin $6,000, software, insurance, and marketing $5,000): $14,000
- Operating income: $22,000 - $14,000 = $8,000, so operating margin = $8,000 / $40,000 = 20 percent
- Variable costs: $18,000 direct costs plus $2,000 in payment fees and per-job fuel = $20,000
- Contribution: $40,000 - $20,000 = $20,000, so contribution margin = $20,000 / $40,000 = 50 percent

Same month, same books, three margins. If the owner says our margin is 55 percent and the bookkeeper says it is 20 percent, neither is wrong. They are describing different layers of the same business, and any calculator output needs a label saying which layer it measured.

## Which margin belongs in an automation decision

**Automation decisions compare the cost of a tool against the profit it protects or creates, and that comparison almost always runs on contribution margin. Use gross margin for pricing questions and operating margin for overhead questions. Feeding the wrong margin into a payback calculation can overstate or understate the return by a wide spread.**

Use this decision order:

1. The tool cuts variable cost per job, such as less direct labor or fewer wasted materials. Value the savings at the variable cost avoided per job times monthly job count. This changes contribution margin.
2. The tool removes fixed overhead, such as salaried admin hours. The savings lift operating income dollar for dollar, so operating margin is the right frame.
3. The tool recovers revenue, such as fewer missed calls or faster lead follow-up. Value each recovered sale at its contribution margin, never at full revenue. A recovered $250 job at a 50 percent contribution margin is worth $125, not $250.

## A margin calculator checklist

**Before trusting a margin number from any calculator, confirm five things: which costs it treats as direct, whether payment and delivery fees are included, the time period used, whether owner salary sits in the expenses, and which margin type the output represents. Skipping these checks is how two people calculate different margins from identical books.**

Run through this list each time:

- [ ] Label every cost as variable or fixed before entering anything
- [ ] Include merchant fees and per-job travel in variable costs, not overhead
- [ ] Match the revenue period and the cost period exactly
- [ ] Decide whether owner pay sits in operating expenses, and keep that choice consistent
- [ ] Write the margin type next to every result so future you knows what the number meant

## When a margin calculator is not enough

**A margin calculator produces a period average, and averages break when the business is lumpy. Seasonal revenue, a product mix with very different margins, or one large atypical job can make a single monthly margin misleading. In those cases, calculate margin per product line or per job before making any spending decision.**

There is also a real limit on the automation side: if a process has thin contribution margin, automating it saves little even when the tool works perfectly. A 10 percent margin process that costs $500 a month to automate rarely pays back. Prioritize high-margin, high-volume work first, and treat capacity constraints separately, because a margin figure says nothing about whether you have the hours to deliver more volume.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between margin and markup?

Margin divides profit by revenue. Markup divides profit by cost. A 50 percent markup produces a 33 percent gross margin. Entering a markup where a calculator asks for margin will distort every downstream decision, so check which one the tool expects.

### Which margin should I use in an automation business case?

Contribution margin in most cases, because automations usually change variable costs or recover incremental sales. Use operating margin only when the tool removes fixed overhead, and use gross margin only for pricing questions.

### Is a higher gross margin always better?

Higher helps, but a business with a 60 percent gross margin can still lose money if overhead absorbs it. Check operating margin for the full picture and contribution margin for any one-more-sale decision.

### How often should I recalculate margins?

Quarterly at minimum, and immediately after any price change, wage change, or new product line. Margins drift quietly, and an automation decision built on last year's margin can point the wrong way.

### Does contribution margin include fixed costs?

No. Contribution margin subtracts only variable costs. Fixed costs get covered by the total contribution across all sales, which is exactly why contribution margin is the right number for incremental decisions.
