**Employee turnover rate equals separations during a period divided by average headcount for that same period, times 100. Run it monthly, quarterly, and annually, then split it by cohort: tenure band, department, and voluntary versus involuntary. The blended company number hides the problem. The cohort number tells you where to look.**

## The turnover rate formula

**Turnover rate = (separations in period / average headcount in period) x 100. Average headcount is (headcount at start + headcount at end) / 2, not your headcount on the last day. Count every separation, voluntary and involuntary. Then keep a second version that excludes involuntary exits, because the two numbers drive different decisions.**

The denominator is where most spreadsheets go wrong. If you grew from 18 to 30 people and divide separations by 30, you understate turnover. If you shrank and divide by the starting number, you overstate it. Averaging the endpoints is the standard fix for a stable period. If your headcount moved a lot mid-period, average the month-end headcounts instead.

Here are the six variants worth keeping in one tab of your workbook.

| Metric | Formula | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Overall turnover rate | (all separations / average headcount) x 100 | Total churn load on the business |
| Voluntary turnover rate | (resignations / average headcount) x 100 | Whether people are choosing to leave |
| Involuntary turnover rate | (terminations / average headcount) x 100 | Whether you are hiring the wrong people |
| New hire turnover rate | (separations within 90 days / hires in period) x 100 | Selection and onboarding fit |
| Retention rate | (people present at both start and end / headcount at start) x 100 | Stability of the team you already had |
| Annualized monthly rate | monthly rate x 12 | A rough forward estimate, nothing more |

Retention rate is not the inverse of turnover rate. You can lose 30 percent of your seats and still retain 90 percent of the people you started with, if the churn concentrates in newly filled roles.

## Choose the period before you compare anything

**Turnover rate has no meaning without a period attached. A 3 percent monthly rate and a 3 percent annual rate describe completely different companies. Pick monthly for operational tracking, quarterly for trend, and annual for benchmarking. When you annualize a single month, label it as an estimate, because one month rarely repeats twelve times.**

Monthly tracking catches problems while you can still act on them. Say you average 24 employees and lose 2 people in March. That is 8.3 percent for the month. Multiplying by 12 gives 100 percent annualized, which is almost certainly wrong, because March may have been the quarter's worst month.

The honest annual number sums twelve months of separations and divides by the average of the twelve month-end headcounts:

Annual turnover = (sum of monthly separations / average of 12 month-end headcounts) x 100

Use annualized monthly figures for early warning and the true annual figure for any comparison. If you are also translating workload into positions, the [FTE calculator method for turning workload into real capacity](/blog/fte-calculator/) keeps your headcount denominator consistent with how you actually staff.

## Cohort turnover is where the signal lives

**A single company-wide rate averages your stable veterans with your revolving front line and produces a number that describes neither. Cut separations by tenure band, department, manager, and exit type. The cohort that produces most of your exits is usually small, recent, and concentrated in one or two roles.**

The four cuts that pay for themselves:

- **Tenure band.** 0 to 90 days, 91 days to 1 year, 1 to 3 years, 3 years and up. Heavy churn in the first band is a hiring and onboarding issue. Heavy churn in the third band is a pay or advancement issue.
- **Department or role.** Front desk turnover and technician turnover have different causes and different fixes.
- **Manager.** Same role, two supervisors, two very different rates is one of the fastest diagnostics available.
- **Exit type.** Voluntary exits point at retention. Involuntary exits point at selection.

Departments with lopsided churn also distort your output per head, which is why it is worth reading turnover alongside the [revenue per employee operating leverage read](/blog/revenue-per-employee-calculator/) rather than in isolation.

## A worked example for a 24-person company

**Illustrative numbers only. A 24-person home services company starts the year at 22 people, ends at 26, and records 9 separations. Average headcount is 24, so annual turnover is 37.5 percent. That headline number is nearly useless until you split it, and the split changes what you fix first.**

Working backward, hires must equal 13, since 22 plus hires minus 9 separations equals 26. Now split the 9 exits.

| Cohort | Denominator | Separations | Rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| New hires, first 90 days | 13 hires | 5 | 38% |
| Field techs past 90 days | 14 avg headcount | 3 | 21% |
| Office and admin | 10 avg headcount | 1 | 10% |

The new hire row uses hires as its denominator, so it does not double count against the two headcount rows, which cover exits after 90 days.

Read the split and the story changes. The company does not have a 37.5 percent retention problem. It has a first-90-days problem that accounts for 5 of 9 exits, plus a field tech cohort running twice the office rate. The office is fine. Chasing a company-wide engagement initiative here would spend budget on the group that was never leaving.

## When the rate misleads you

**Small denominators make the rate jumpy. In a 12-person shop, one exit swings the annual rate by more than 8 points, which is noise, not a trend. The rate also says nothing about money. It counts bodies leaving, not the margin those departures cost you.**

Three more traps worth naming. Seasonal businesses that hire and release crews on purpose will show high turnover that is not a problem at all, so exclude planned seasonal separations or report them on a separate line. Fast-growing companies show artificially low rates because the denominator inflates faster than exits accumulate. And a one-time restructure should be footnoted, not blended into the trend.

Most important: rate is a frequency, not a cost. Once you know how often people leave, price what each departure actually costs using the [five real buckets in the cost of employee turnover calculator](/blog/cost-of-employee-turnover-calculator/), and value the lost productive hours at your true [fully loaded labor rate](/blog/fully-loaded-labor-rate/) rather than base wage. Rate tells you where. Cost tells you whether it is worth fixing.

## Your monthly tracking checklist

**Track turnover the same way every month or the trend line lies. Fix your definitions once, write them down, and apply them to every period. The seven steps below take about 20 minutes a month once your separation log carries the four fields it needs.**

1. Log every separation with hire date, exit date, department, and exit type.
2. Record month-start and month-end headcount on the same day each month.
3. Calculate average headcount as the average of those two numbers.
4. Compute overall, voluntary, and involuntary rates for the month.
5. Compute new hire turnover using hires as the denominator, not headcount.
6. Update a rolling 12-month annual rate so one bad month does not read as a trend.
7. Flag any cohort running more than double the company rate and investigate that one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What counts as a separation?

Every employee who leaves the payroll during the period: resignations, terminations, layoffs, retirements, and deaths. Internal transfers and promotions are not separations, since the person is still employed. Keep contractors out of the calculation entirely unless you track them as a separate population with their own rate.

### Should I include involuntary terminations in my turnover rate?

Include them in the overall rate and also report them separately. Overall turnover shows total disruption to operations. Voluntary turnover shows whether people are choosing to leave. Involuntary turnover usually points at hiring or onboarding, and a fix there is different from a retention fix.

### What is a good employee turnover rate?

There is no universal target, because rates vary widely by industry, role, and wage level. The useful comparison is your own trend and your own cohorts. If one department runs double another for the same tenure band, that gap is actionable regardless of any external benchmark.

### How do I calculate turnover when headcount changed a lot?

Average the month-end headcounts across the period rather than averaging only the first and last values. With twelve monthly readings, the denominator reflects the staffing you actually carried, so a hiring surge in month eleven does not distort the whole year.

### Is turnover rate the same as attrition rate?

They are often used interchangeably, but many companies reserve attrition for positions that are not backfilled and turnover for seats that get refilled. Whichever definition you use, write it down and keep it constant, because switching definitions mid-year breaks every comparison you make.
