**A fully loaded receptionist typically costs $45,000 to $58,000 a year once you add payroll taxes, benefits, and turnover risk to the base wage. Most AI receptionist software runs $200 to $1,500 a month depending on call volume. For businesses under a few hundred call-minutes a month, AI is usually cheaper on paper, but a person absorbs edge cases, judgment calls, and physical tasks that software cannot.**

## What a fully loaded receptionist actually costs

**A $19-an-hour receptionist rarely costs $19 an hour to employ. After payroll taxes, benefits, and basic overhead, the fully loaded rate typically lands between $25 and $28 an hour, pushing annual cost for one full-time front-desk hire to roughly $50,000 to $58,000.**

Here is an illustrative build for a full-time receptionist working 40 hours a week at $19 an hour base pay.

| Cost component | Illustrative annual amount |
|---|---|
| Base wage ($19/hr x 2,080 hrs) | $39,520 |
| Payroll taxes (FICA + unemployment, ~9.5%) | $3,754 |
| Benefits (health, PTO, ~18%) | $7,114 |
| Overhead (workstation, phone line, software seats, ~5%) | $1,976 |
| **Fully loaded annual total** | **$52,364** |
| **Fully loaded monthly total** | **$4,364** |

That's before recruiting cost, onboarding time, or the gap coverage you pay for during sick days, vacation, and the average 60 to 90 days it takes to fill an open seat. For a deeper breakdown of how the loaded multiplier works, see [fully loaded labor rate: how to calculate yours](/blog/fully-loaded-labor-rate). ## What AI receptionist software actually costs

**AI receptionist tools are priced three ways: flat monthly plans, per-minute usage, or per-call fees, and most small businesses land between $300 and $900 a month for a few hundred minutes of handled calls. There is no payroll tax, no benefits line, and no coverage gap when volume spikes or a shift goes unfilled.**

Pricing varies more by vendor than by feature set, which is why the underlying model matters more than the sticker price. A breakdown of what each pricing structure charges and where the hidden costs sit is in [AI receptionist cost: what the three pricing models charge](/blog/ai-receptionist-cost). If your call volume is uneven month to month, overage fees can erase the savings fast; that math is covered in [virtual receptionist pricing: minutes, calls, and overages](/blog/virtual-receptionist-pricing).

## Side-by-side: the monthly numbers

**At typical small-business call volumes, AI receptionist software costs roughly one-fifth to one-half of a fully loaded human hire per month. The gap narrows as call volume climbs into the thousands of minutes, where per-minute overage charges can push AI pricing close to, or past, a single receptionist's loaded rate.**

| Scenario | Fully loaded human receptionist | AI receptionist software (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|
| Low volume (under 300 min/month) | $4,364/month | $250 to $450/month |
| Moderate volume (300 to 1,000 min/month) | $4,364/month | $450 to $900/month |
| High volume (2,000+ min/month) | $4,364/month | $1,200 to $2,500/month (plan-dependent) |
| After-hours and weekend coverage | Overtime or a second hire | Usually included in the base plan |

At low and moderate volumes, the AI option wins on cost by a wide margin. At high volume, the comparison depends heavily on which pricing model you're on, which is why checking the per-minute or per-call rate before signing matters more than the advertised base price.

## The exceptions a person absorbs that software doesn't

**A receptionist does more than answer the phone: they read a room, make judgment calls on ambiguous requests, notice when something is off, and pick up whatever administrative task lands on the desk that day. None of that shows up in a pricing comparison, but all of it has real value.**

Common exceptions a person handles that most AI receptionist tools still cannot:

- **Walk-in traffic.** Someone at the counter needs a face, not a phone tree.
- **Ambiguous or emotionally charged calls.** A caller who is upset, confused, or vague often needs a human to read tone and improvise, not follow a script.
- **Physical tasks.** Sorting mail, restocking the front desk, signing for deliveries, and other small jobs that pile up during the day.
- **Cross-trained overflow work.** Filing, light bookkeeping, scheduling for other staff, or covering a second role during slow stretches.
- **Relationship memory.** A repeat customer who wants to be recognized, not re-verified every call.
- **Security and fraud judgment.** Spotting a suspicious visitor or a scam call that doesn't match any script pattern.

None of these are dealbreakers for AI receptionist software, but each one is a cost you're implicitly paying for when you keep a person on staff, and a gap you're implicitly accepting when you don't.

## When hiring still wins

**Hiring still makes sense when the role needs a physical presence at a front desk, regularly absorbs overflow admin work beyond answering calls, or when high-ticket, high-touch sales conversations benefit from a person building rapport in real time. AI receptionist software is built for call handling, not for everything else a receptionist quietly does.**

This is the honest limitation: if you're comparing pure phone-answering cost, AI usually wins below a few hundred calls a month. If the receptionist role in your business is really "office generalist who also answers phones," swapping in software only solves part of the job, and you may end up paying for both the software and a part-time person to cover the rest.

## How to decide for your business

**Run the comparison on your actual numbers, not the illustrative ones above, before deciding. Pull your current call volume, your fully loaded hire cost, and a real quote from an AI receptionist vendor, then compare them side by side using the same monthly time frame.**

1. Calculate your fully loaded hourly and monthly cost for the role using your actual wage, benefits, and overhead figures.
2. Pull three months of call logs to get a realistic average minutes-per-month number, including seasonal spikes.
3. Get quotes from two or three AI receptionist vendors using your real call volume, not a sales demo estimate.
4. List which non-phone tasks the current or future receptionist role covers, and price what it would cost to cover those separately.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is an AI receptionist always cheaper than a human hire?

Not always. Below a few hundred call-minutes a month, AI receptionist software is almost always cheaper than a fully loaded hire. At very high call volumes, or when the role covers significant non-phone work, the cost gap narrows or can reverse depending on the vendor's pricing model.

### What does "fully loaded cost" mean for a receptionist hire?

It means base wage plus payroll taxes, benefits, and overhead like workstation and software costs, not just the hourly wage. A $19-an-hour receptionist typically costs $25 to $28 an hour once those additions are included.

### Can AI receptionist software handle walk-in customers?

No. AI receptionist tools handle phone calls and, in some cases, text or chat. They do not cover a physical front desk, so businesses with regular walk-in traffic usually still need a person on-site.

### Do AI receptionists cost more as call volume grows?

Usually, yes, if you're on a per-minute or per-call plan. Flat monthly plans cap the cost but often come with volume limits and overage fees once you exceed them, so check the pricing structure before comparing it to a hire.

### Should I replace my receptionist entirely or run both?

Many small businesses run AI receptionist software for after-hours and overflow calls while keeping a person for front-desk duties and complex requests. Comparing both options against your actual call volume and task list will show whether a full replacement or a hybrid setup saves more.
