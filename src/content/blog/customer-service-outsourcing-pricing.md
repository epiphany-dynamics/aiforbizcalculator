**Outsourced customer service is quoted five different ways: per agent, per hour, per ticket, per resolution, or on a retainer. None of those numbers are comparable to each other, to in-house staffing, or to automation until you convert all of them into cost per resolved contact. That conversion is the whole job.**

Most owners collect three outsourcing quotes, line them up in a spreadsheet, and pick the lowest headline number. The headline number is the least useful figure on the page. A $2,400 seat and a $1.80 ticket price can end up at the same real cost, or one can be double the other, depending entirely on ramp, minimums, premiums, and how many contacts come back a second time.

## How outsourcing vendors actually structure a price

**Vendors price on five common structures, and each one shifts a different risk onto you. Seat pricing pushes utilization risk your way. Per ticket pushes quality risk your way, because a bad answer bills twice. Per resolution sounds safest until you read who gets to define resolved. Retainers hide the overage rate.**

| Structure | Unit charged | Common minimum | What it hides from you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Per agent (dedicated) | Monthly seat | 1 to 3 seats, term commitment | Idle time you pay for; filling the seat is your problem |
| Per hour | Billable hour | Monthly hour block | Whether breaks, meetings, and training bill |
| Per ticket or per contact | Each contact handled | Monthly contact floor | Re-contacts bill again; poor first answers pay the vendor twice |
| Per resolution | Each closed contact | Resolution definition | Who marks resolved, and whether escalations back to you still bill |
| Retainer or blended | Fixed monthly fee | Volume band | The overage rate above the band |

Ask every vendor for their structure in writing plus the three numbers that make it readable: the minimum, the overage rate, and their definition of a handled versus resolved contact. If a vendor will not define resolved, treat their per-resolution quote as per-ticket pricing with better marketing.

## The line items that move a quote 20 to 40 percent

**The base rate is rarely the final rate. Ramp, QA supervision, language and channel premiums, minimums, overage, and technology pass-through all land on top. Build your comparison from the loaded monthly cost, not the quoted seat or ticket rate, or you will systematically underestimate every outsourced option.**

Work through this checklist on each quote before you compare anything:

1. **Ramp and training.** One-time onboarding fees, plus the weeks where output is below target. Amortize the fee across your committed term.
2. **QA and team lead load.** Often 8 to 15 percent on top of seat cost, sometimes bundled, sometimes not. Confirm which.
3. **Language premium.** Bilingual or non-English coverage typically carries a per-seat uplift.
4. **Channel premium.** Voice usually prices above chat and email. After-hours, weekend, and holiday coverage price above weekday.
5. **Minimums and term.** A three-seat minimum on a one-seat need is a 200 percent effective markup.
6. **Overage rate.** The number that matters in your busiest month, not your average one.
7. **Technology pass-through.** Whose helpdesk and telephony seats are you paying for? Compare against your own stack using the [call center software pricing first-year total](/blog/call-center-software-pricing/).
8. **Compliance overhead.** Regulated intake changes the price floor. The [medical answering service pricing breakdown of what HIPAA adds](/blog/medical-answering-service-pricing/) shows how much a BAA and trained staff move a per-call rate.
9. **Your own management time.** Somebody internal runs the weekly call, reviews QA samples, and writes the escalation rules. Price that hour at your [fully loaded labor rate](/blog/fully-loaded-labor-rate/).

## Normalize everything to cost per resolved contact

**Use one denominator for every option. Loaded monthly cost divided by resolved contacts. Resolved contacts are handled contacts minus repeat contacts about the same issue minus escalations you personally closed. Anything else flatters the vendor, because a contact that comes back three times counts three times in a handled-volume denominator.**

The two formulas:

```
Loaded monthly cost = base fee + overage + premiums + (ramp / term months)
                      + QA fees + tech pass-through + your management hours

Resolved contacts   = handled contacts - repeat contacts (same issue, 7 days)
                      - escalations you closed yourself

Cost per resolved contact = loaded monthly cost / resolved contacts
```

### A worked example, illustrative numbers only

The figures below are invented for arithmetic, not benchmarks. Substitute your own quote.

- 3 dedicated seats at $2,400: **$7,200**
- Ramp fee $4,500 amortized over a 12 month term: **$375**
- QA and team lead at 10 percent of seat cost: **$720**
- Spanish premium on one seat: **$300**
- Weekend and after-hours coverage: **$600**
- Helpdesk and telephony pass-through, 3 seats at $120: **$360**
- Your management time, 6 hours at $65 loaded: **$390**
- **Loaded monthly cost: $9,945**

Volume: 3,000 contacts handled, 240 repeat contacts inside 7 days, 210 escalations you closed. Resolved contacts: 2,550.

Cost per resolved contact: **$9,945 / 2,550 = $3.90**.

The headline seat math would have told you $7,200 / 3,000 = $2.40. The honest number is 63 percent higher, and none of that gap is the vendor cheating. It is just the part of the quote nobody totals. The same denominator discipline applies to your internal baseline, which is why the [cost per contact formula and the deflection trap](/blog/cost-per-contact/) is worth reading before you build the comparison.

## Comparing outsourced, in-house, and automation on one line

**Only after normalizing does the comparison mean anything. Below, all three options carry the same 3,000 monthly contacts and the same loaded-cost rules. Automation looks cheapest, but its number is the most sensitive to one assumption: containment. Get containment wrong and the ranking flips.**

| Option (illustrative, 3,000 contacts) | Loaded monthly cost | Resolved contacts | Cost per resolved |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outsourced, 3 dedicated seats | $9,945 | 2,550 | $3.90 |
| In-house, 3 agents plus supervisor share | $16,760 | 2,850 | $5.88 |
| Automation plus 1 in-house agent | $6,920 | 2,750 | $2.52 |

The automation row assumes the system cleanly resolves half of contacts. Drop containment to 25 percent and your single agent cannot absorb the remainder. You either add a seat, which pushes the row past $4.00, or you lose resolutions, which pushes it past both alternatives once you count the revenue attached to an abandoned contact. That sensitivity is the entire argument in [the honest answer on whether AI will replace customer service](https://epiphanydynamics.ai/blog/will-ai-replace-customer-service/).

## When outsourcing is the wrong call

**Outsourcing prices well on volume and prices badly on complexity. If your contacts require judgment, account history, or knowledge that lives in one person's head, per-contact rates climb, ramp stretches from weeks to months, and escalations back to you eat the savings. Under roughly 300 monthly contacts, minimums usually make it the worst option available.**

Three specific situations where the math will not work:

- **Low volume against a seat minimum.** Paying for three seats to fill one is a markup, not a service.
- **High-variance, high-stakes contacts.** Sales-adjacent conversations, disputes, and clinical intake carry a cost of getting it wrong that no per-ticket rate captures.
- **No documented process.** Vendors execute a runbook. If you do not have one, you are paying agency rates for someone to write it, and your ramp cost triples.

## How to decide in one sitting

**Get every quote in a comparable form, total the loaded cost, divide by resolved contacts, and compare the three rows. If two options land within 15 percent of each other, choose on flexibility and exit terms instead of price, because your volume forecast is less accurate than that gap.**

The order that works:

1. Pull last quarter's contact volume, repeat rate, and channel mix from your helpdesk.
2. Total your current internal loaded cost and compute your baseline cost per resolved contact.
3. Convert each vendor quote to loaded monthly cost using the nine-item checklist above.
4. Divide all three by resolved contacts, not handled contacts.
5. Stress test the automation row at half the containment you were promised.
6. Compare exit terms. A 10 percent price premium for month-to-month is usually worth paying in year one.

Once you have the three rows, put them into the [AI automation ROI calculator](/calculator/) with your own volume and payback window. The output that matters is not which option is cheapest this month, it is which one is still cheapest when volume moves 30 percent in either direction.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is per-resolution pricing better than per-ticket pricing?

Usually, because it aligns the vendor with the outcome instead of the activity. The value depends entirely on the contract definition of resolved. If the vendor can mark a contact resolved by escalating it to you, per-resolution pricing is per-ticket pricing with a friendlier label. Require that escalations to your team do not count as resolutions.

### What minimum commitment is normal?

Dedicated seat models commonly carry a seat minimum plus a term of six to twelve months. Shared and per-ticket models carry a monthly volume or dollar floor instead. Neither is unreasonable, since the vendor is amortizing their own ramp cost, but always compute your effective rate at your realistic low month, not your average month.

### How much should I budget for ramp?

Budget both the stated onboarding fee and the productivity gap during the first four to eight weeks. Amortize the fee across your committed term rather than expensing it in month one, otherwise your first-month cost per resolved contact looks alarming and your steady-state number looks better than it is.

### Does outsourcing ever beat automation on price?

Yes, on complex or low-containment work. Automation wins where contacts are repetitive and well documented. Outsourcing wins where contacts require judgment that is expensive to encode. Many operations end up running both, with automation handling the top five repeat questions and humans owning the rest, which is why comparing the two on cost per resolved contact matters more than picking a side.

### How do I count a resolved contact if my helpdesk does not track repeats?

Approximate it. Pull contacts from the same customer within a seven-day window on the same channel and treat them as one contact for the denominator. It will slightly undercount genuine new issues, which makes it a conservative estimate, and conservative is the correct direction when you are about to sign a term commitment.
